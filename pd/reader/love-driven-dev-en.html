Overview

BackgroundOver this year, pjdhiro has explored the shapes things take as they come into being, and human consciousness. Along the way, a question remained: what moves them? pjdhiro has also seen people's minds acting on one another unconsciously, and movements of emotion that do not proceed by logic. (For more, see "How I came to this question" at the end.)

The research cited is lined up not to prove the experience but as material for conveying it. Answer for nowWe may be inside something like a field we want to call Love, and part of it. The body senses the reassurance of being connected, and gaps from it. Feeling those gaps as a lack, people start to move. The driving source of projects may be this lack, the gap between what I want to call Love and oneself. Part 1

The question, and the views so far

What drives a project?

After a year of exploring the shapes things take as they come into being, and human consciousness, one question remained.

pjdhiro's question (translated by Claude):What drives me to do projects? What drives me to live?

Businesses and projects are usually told starting from a vision. Yet looking back, it can feel as though something had begun to move before the vision was put into words. Even the people making something sometimes cannot tell whether they are making it or it is arising from what surrounds them. On this page, whatever moves people and projects is called the driving source, and the page looks for what it is.

More — what starts moving before a vision is put into words

The movement before a vision is put into words takes several forms. Within one person, dots may connect afterward, as if preparation had already begun. Often the field itself is fluctuating, as with social movements and trends, and people start to move riding that fluctuation, or sometimes betting against it. People being drawn together and something beginning is one case among these.

About the results of design and vision, pjdhiro feels the following.

pjdhiro's experience (translated by Claude):…It seems as if I am trying to make it, but it is born as if the field were swirling into a vortex. I feel so many driving sources that I cannot tell whether it is me or the environment. Is it causation? Is it correlation?

More — the path management studies have written, and what is not yet known

Management studies and project management have described in detail the path that follows once a vision has been put into words: set the purpose, make a plan, gather people and resources, check progress and correct course. Such descriptions work as they are in many situations. Which way of thinking has treated what as the driving source is listed in the next chapter, "What has management studies seen as the driving source?".

About the vision itself, the 5W1H are still not well understood: why it arises, and where, in whom, when, from what and how. Whether business opportunities are discovered or created is still debated in entrepreneurship research (in 2000 Shane and Venkataraman framed the field around the discovery, evaluation and exploitation of opportunities, and in 2007 Alvarez and Barney set side by side two views, discovery and creation).

Looking back, many people have experienced dots connecting before a vision, as if preparation had already begun, or being moved by fluctuations in the field. What Love-Driven Development tries to see is something like a law that works on people, beyond their control, behind such things happening. For now it approaches the "why" of this from the angle of the driving source.

What has management studies seen as the driving source?

When explaining Love-Driven Development, pjdhiro has put answering the following question at the core of the explanation.

pjdhiro's question (translated by Claude):What is the driving source of a project?

Management studies has seen the driving source in many places: markets and competition, a company's resources, goals and plans, learning from action, organizational culture, people's motivation. Each way of thinking works well in situations that suit it. Yet laid on top of one another, they feel like the parable of the blind men and the elephant: each surely touches part of the elephant, and still something seems missing. Going down from the outside inward, one finally arrives at the entrepreneur's mind. What Love-Driven Development follows is what lies further behind that mind and moves it.

What it is directed at also differs from source to source. One particular person, someone with whom one wishes each other good, those extended in order from the near, all living beings without boundaries, God, things that are not human, oneself, the makeup of the world.

Ren'ai (romantic love) is a word used as the translation of love from the beginning of the Meiji era. Psychology has divided love into measurable forms such as behavior, states and components. Neuroscience research measures the brain activity of people in love, but does not write that some part of the brain is the seat of love.

What shape does creation take?

Part 2 looks at the invisible driving source through the structure of how things come into being. The exploration that describes that structure is "What Is Creation". The shape of creation continues as a basic viewpoint into the second half of this reader, so an overview is given first.

"What Is Creation" holds that creation proceeds field → wave → edge → vortex → bundle. Its top page sets out the five stages like this: "The field is the environment and conditions in which creation arises; the wave is the process of exploration and fluctuation; the edge is the boundary where heterogeneous elements meet; the vortex is the core stage in which elements combine and transform; the bundle is the completion in which they are integrated as a new form" (translated by Claude).

The trough of the edge corresponds to the border where heterogeneous things meet, and the ocean current of the bundle to a gathering that remains as a new form and becomes the next field.

Here are examples from five domains where similar shapes have been reported. The sources do not write in five stages; arranging the descriptions of the theories in the order of the five stages is Claude's reading.

  • Physics (dissipative structures, with Bénard convection as the example): The field is a layer of liquid heated from below. Small fluctuations away from the average state keep arising and dying out. The wave is some fluctuations growing once the temperature gradient exceeds a certain value. The edge is the contest between buoyancy and the viscosity and heat conduction that hold it back; once the value is exceeded, of the many patterns of disturbance, the one that grows fastest wins out. The vortex is the grown fluctuation becoming an order sustained by exchange of energy with the outside, the layer dividing into cells. The bundle is scattered polygons slowly settling into a network of regular hexagons.
  • Biochemistry (two, from life science and chemistry):
    • Fertilization (life science; from research on mouse zygotes): The field is the inside of the egg where fertilization happens. The wave is the half genomes (maternal and paternal) that the two gametes, split into female and male, the egg and the sperm, each bring. The edge is the chromosomes of the two genomes meeting for the first time when the nuclear membrane breaks down and the first division begins. The vortex is a spindle forming around each of the two genomes, the two lining up and carrying out one division together while the two genomes remain distinguishable without mixing. The bundle is becoming a two-cell embryo, and the two genomes mixing in the following divisions to become the genome of a new individual.
    • Chemistry (the BZ reaction): The field is the solution in one vessel (bromate ions, an organic acid and a catalyst dissolved in it). The wave is two substances competing for an intermediate. The edge is the switch to another process when one falls below a critical concentration, a process that indirectly brings it back. The vortex is neither process winning outright, and the back-and-forth remaining as a stable form. The bundle is arriving at the same single periodic orbit regardless of the initial state.
  • Psychology (moving between good and bad aspects; Klein): The field is the infant's mind. The wave is love and hate splitting between times of being satisfied and not, and protecting oneself from the terrifying aspect by enlarging the good aspect (idealization). The edge is the two aspects held apart colliding, and fear of loss, a sense of guilt and feelings close to mourning arising. The vortex is the loved aspect and the hated aspect being felt as belonging to one person who is not split apart. The urge to repair the one one feels one has harmed (reparation) comes to the fore, trust is regained little by little, and while bearing the knowledge that the other was not perfect, one can keep trust and love. The bundle is the splitting movement remaining in changed form, and anxious situations being experienced again and again.
  • A plain example (romantic love): The field is two people who do not know each other, each in their own life. The wave is the two meeting and being attracted to each other. The edge is, the closer they get, differences in ways of thinking and living, and the difference between the partner one pictured and the partner in front of one, colliding. The vortex is the two becoming as if one while each remains a different person. The bundle is the life and promises the two built up together remaining and becoming the field of the days to come. This flow was not built from research; Claude wrote it as a plain form (research on couples at the start of marriage is in the fold below).
  • Management studies (innovation, and the evolution of organizations):
    • Innovation (Schumpeter's new combinations): The field is the circular flow of an economy repeating familiar routes. The wave is the person who tries to leave the familiar route and do something new, the resistance of surrounding society, and resistance within oneself. The edge is pulling means of production away from old uses and fighting competitors head on. The vortex is newly combining and using what was pulled away (new combinations). The bundle is new combinations occurring in swarms, disrupting the existing system and forcing adaptation, with revolution and the absorption of its results alternating.
    • Innovation (organizational knowledge creation, Nonaka et al.): The field is the organization and the project (months to years). The wave is pairs that look contradictory, such as tacit and explicit knowledge, or order and chaos. The edge is routines, habits and cognitive frameworks breaking down through change from outside or difficult challenges from above (fluctuation), and a sense of crisis arising. The vortex is a concept being born in dialogue and, after justification, becoming an archetype. The bundle is the archetype moving to another department or level and starting the next cycle.
    • The evolution of organizations (March's exploration and exploitation): The field is the organization's knowledge (the code) and the individuals belonging to it. The wave is the exploration of new possibilities and the exploitation of known certainties. The edge is the clash of gains between the short and long term, and between individual and organizational knowledge; the code can learn only from individuals who deviate from it. The vortex is slow-learning individuals and turnover keeping diversity, and the knowledge of the code rising. The bundle is the balance between exploration and exploitation being kept, and the organization surviving and prospering.

Cases that departed from each shape are also on record. In Bénard convection, if the temperature gradient is smaller than the dividing value, fluctuations die out and things return to how they were. In a zygote, if the two spindles fail to line up, the result is an embryo of cells with two nuclei. In the BZ reaction, when conditions are off, no oscillation happens and it settles into a steady state. In the wave of new combinations, businesses that cannot adapt may perish. An organization leaning only toward exploration scatters immature ideas; one leaning only toward exploitation is trapped in a suboptimal stability.

More — the sources of the five examples, and the records

The records for each domain are in the domain records of What Is Creation (in Japanese); a window listing the 30 domains opens. They arrange what is written as shapes similar to the five stages. The reading of whether they are similar belongs to the records; here, the description stays within what the sources say.

pjdhiro chose the five domains and the theme for each; Claude chose which theories to take up within each theme. In the records for each domain, the opposition, collision, integration or new stability, and unit of each theory are written in separate columns, and the five-stage sequences in the main text lay those columns out in order. Every theory taken up was checked against the full text of the source, and most have been checked in the domain records or in the research for the sister reader "What Is Psychological Safety".

Physics (dissipative structures). Prigogine (1977, Nobel lecture) describes systems far from equilibrium that exchange with their outside. Fluctuations away from the average state are always present; when the temperature gradient or the like is smaller than a certain value they are damped and vanish, and when it exceeds that value some fluctuations are amplified into macroscopic order. The new order is stabilized by exchange of energy with the outside (p.267). Near the branching point, fluctuations decide which branch the system goes down (p.273), and in a finite system fluctuations eventually disturb the direction of the order (p.277). On Bénard convection itself, Rayleigh (1916) writes that until the temperature gradient exceeds a certain value the equilibrium is stable, and once it does, of the several patterns of disturbance the one with the greatest growth rate predominates (pp.530, 539), and Bénard (1901) writes that what was at first a network of polygons with four to seven sides slowly settles into regular hexagons (p.6). However, Rayleigh writes that the theory's boundary conditions at top and bottom differ from Bénard's experiment, and that it does not deal with the transition to hexagons (pp.532–533). It is also written that in a liquid of extremely low viscosity, the vortical flow does not settle (p.4). These are theory 8 in the physics record and theory 1 in the complexity science record.

Life science (fertilization). Reichmann et al. (2018) begin by writing that after fertilization the half genomes of egg and sperm come together to become the genome of a new individual, and that in mammals the paternal and maternal chromosomes first meet when the nuclear membrane breaks down and the first division begins (p.3). Live imaging of mouse zygotes showed the two genomes staying apart throughout the first division (p.3). A spindle forms around each of the two pronuclei, each gathering one of the genomes. The two spindles line up and align their poles before division, but the two genomes are separated without mixing (pp.3–5, 7). When the two spindles fail to line up, the result is an embryo of cells with two nuclei, the same form as an error often seen in in-vitro fertilization clinics (pp.5–6). The two genomes stay apart only during the first division; in the following divisions they mix under one spindle (p.6).

Chemistry (the BZ reaction). The Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction is a chemical oscillation in which the color of the solution changes periodically. In the model of Field and Noyes (1974), bromide ions and bromate ions compete for bromous acid; when bromide ions fall below a critical concentration, the system switches to an autocatalytic process, which indirectly produces bromide and brings things back. This back-and-forth arrives at the orbit of the same period regardless of the initial state. It is also written that when the coefficients are off, no oscillation occurs and the system settles into a steady state. This is theory 3 in the chemistry record.

Psychology (Klein). Klein (1946) writes that the infant holds the satisfying object and the frustrating object apart, and protects itself from the terrifying aspect by enlarging the good aspect (pp.99, 101). As it grows, the loved and hated aspects come to be felt as not apart; fear of loss, a sense of guilt and a state close to mourning arise, and the urge to repair the one it feels it has harmed (reparation) comes to the fore. The splitting movement remains in changed form, and anxious situations are experienced again and again (p.105). Splitting mechanisms and defenses are part of normal development, and also the ground of later illness (p.109). Klein (1940) writes that when hatred prevails in mourning, trust in the good object is shaken and idealization is disturbed (pp.137–138); that as trust is regained little by little, one can bear the knowledge that the object was not perfect and still not lose trust and love (p.138); and that with each step of integration, splitting occurs again (p.132). Both are inferences from the infant mind and from the clinic of mourning, and the 1946 paper notes that these are hypotheses inferred from analysis (p.99). Idealization, and how it differs from trust, are looked at in the chapter "How do idealization and trust differ?".

A plain example (romantic love). The flow of romantic love in the main text was not built from research. What research follows is mainly couples at the start of marriage. The working paper of Niehuis, Reifman and Lee (2013) summarizes research on the line of disillusionment: the view that during courtship partners manage impressions and idealize each other, and on marriage the idealized image gives way to a realistic one; and the report that disillusionment in the first two years of marriage (a decline in love, a decline in affection, a decline in seeing the partner as responsive, an increase in ambivalence) distinguished couples heading for divorce from those who were not (pp.3–6). Murray et al. (2011) measured 222 first-married, childless couples, married two to six months, seven times every half year over three years. Satisfaction clearly declined overall, but those who at the time of marriage strongly saw their partner as matching their ideals did not decline in satisfaction. The authors infer that when time and interdependence reveal that the partner falls short of expectations, people who softly revise their ideals to fit the partner as now seen may keep their satisfaction, and also write that the data are correlational, not proof of causation. What the research followed is couples at the start of marriage, not romantic love as a whole.

Management studies (innovation, Schumpeter). Schumpeter (1912) defines economic development not as a stimulus from outside but as a change in the circular flow that the economy produces "aus sich selbst heraus" (p.103), and writes of development as the carrying out of new combinations. Trying to leave the familiar route runs into the pressure of surrounding society and resistance within oneself ("in seiner eigenen Brust") (pp.117–120), and the person who carries out new combinations fights that resistance (p.132). New combinations are carried out by pulling part of the existing goods away from their previous uses and using them differently (p.158), for example when fighting competitors head on (p.174). In the phase of crisis, businesses that cannot make the needed adaptation may perish (pp.452–453). The 1939 book writes that innovations occur in swarms, disrupt the existing system and force a distinct process of adaptation (pp.100–101), and the 1942 book writes that industrial mutation incessantly revolutionizes the economic structure from within, destroying the old one and creating a new one (p.83). The revolutions are not continuous but occur in discrete rushes separated by spans of comparative quiet, and revolution and the absorption of its results make up the business cycle (note on p.83). This is theory 1 in the economics record.

Management studies (innovation, Nonaka et al.). Nonaka et al. (1996) describe knowledge creation in five phases. Starting from sharing tacit knowledge, it proceeds to creating concepts, justification and building an archetype, and the concept that has become an archetype moves across the boundaries of levels and departments and starts a new cycle of knowledge creation (pp.213–215). When fluctuation enters, the people in the organization face a breakdown of routines, habits and cognitive frameworks, and confusion, and a sense of crisis arises (p.211). It is also written that Japanese-style knowledge creation can fall into groupthink and over-adaptation to past success (pp.215–216). Nonaka et al. (2000) write that knowledge is created in a spiral passing through seemingly opposed pairs such as order and chaos, tacit and explicit, self and other, and that the key is dialectical thinking that synthesizes beyond contradiction (p.7). Creative chaos is intentional chaos, in which leaders set challenging goals to evoke a sense of crisis, and leaders guide the organization so it does not fall into complete disorder (pp.26–27). This is theory 1 in the management studies record.

Management studies (the evolution of organizations). March (1991) writes that in organizational learning the allocation of resources between exploring new possibilities and exploiting known certainties conflicts. Systems leaning only toward exploration have too many undeveloped new ideas and too little distinctive competence, and systems leaning only toward exploitation are likely to be trapped in suboptimal stable equilibria. So maintaining a balance between the two is a primary factor in survival and prosperity (p.71). The trade-off between exploration and exploitation includes conflicts between short-term and long-term concerns, and between gains to individual knowledge and gains to collective knowledge (p.74). The organizational code can learn only from individuals who deviate from it, and slow-learning individuals maintain diversity longer and raise the knowledge of the code (p.76). This theory is a simulation using an organizational code and models of individuals, not a record of evolution itself. This is theory 5 in the management studies record.

More — everyday phenomena and research analyses by domain

For each of the five domains, here are two everyday phenomena a middle-school student can understand, and two analyses whose sources were read in full and checked in the records. Ones not read in the records are left out. "Analysis" here means the content of a source written in the records; no ranking of which is most advanced is given.

Physics. Phenomena: (1) a thin layer of liquid heated from below divides into cells and forms a network of hexagons (Bénard convection). (2) Moist air cools and becomes water droplets (droplets forming from supersaturated vapor). Analyses: (1) Prigogine (1977) writes that until the temperature gradient exceeds the dividing value, fluctuations are damped and vanish, and once it is exceeded, some fluctuations grow into an order sustained by exchange of energy with the outside. (2) Becker and Döring (1935) write that in supersaturated vapor, droplets smaller than a critical size evaporate back, and droplets grow only when a nucleus of critical size is born by fluctuation.

Biochemistry. Phenomena: (1) the color of a solution changes periodically (the BZ reaction). (2) A male and a female meet and offspring are born that inherit from both (fertilization). Analyses: (1) Novák and Tyson (2008) list four conditions for sustained oscillation: negative feedback, time delay, nonlinearity, and a balance of timescales. (2) Reichmann et al. (2018) show, by imaging live embryos, that in mouse zygotes a spindle forms around each of the maternal and paternal genomes, and the two genomes stay apart without mixing throughout the first division.

Psychology. Phenomena: (1) one day you think a close friend is a wonderful person, and on the day you quarrel they feel like a terrible person. (2) After losing someone important, grief, blame and longing arise at the same time. Analyses: (1) Klein (1946) writes that the infant holds the satisfying object and the frustrating object apart, and protects itself from the terrifying aspect by enlarging the good aspect. (2) Klein (1940) writes that when hatred prevails in mourning, trust in the good object is shaken, and as trust is regained little by little, one can bear the knowledge that the object was not perfect.

A plain example (romantic love). Phenomena: (1) at the start of dating the partner looks wonderful, and as time passes their faults stand out. (2) Within a few years of marriage, a couple's satisfaction declines. Analyses: (1) The working paper of Niehuis et al. (2013) summarizes research on the line of disillusionment at the start of marriage as a decline in love and affection and an increase in ambivalence. (2) Lavner and Bradbury (2010) followed 464 newlyweds for four years and write that satisfaction on average starts high and declines, but that there are five groups of trajectories, such as a group that starts high and does not decline, and a group that starts low and declines sharply.

Management studies. Phenomena: (1) a new product is born at the end of confusion on the ground. (2) A company cuts back on new attempts and only polishes its usual strengths. Analyses: (1) Nonaka et al. (2000) write that in developing the Prius, top management rejected a proposal to improve fuel efficiency by 50% and demanded 100%, and after the team went through confusion it developed a hybrid car (p.27). (2) March (1991) writes that leaning only toward exploitation traps one in a suboptimal stable equilibrium.

More — examples from other domains (quantum theory, insight, aesthetics, team transitions)

Shapes similar to the five stages are also in the records of other domains. Here are four. As above, arranging them into the five stages is Claude's reading.

  • Quantum theory (physics): The field is one quantum system and its environment. The wave is quantum superposition and an environment that keeps monitoring the system. The edge is where interaction with the environment destroys interference between selected states. The vortex is only the few states that withstand the environment's monitoring remaining. The bundle is records of the remaining states being copied many times over in the environment.
  • Psychology (insight): The field is inside one person's head (seconds to minutes). The wave is a way of seeing that comes from past experience, and a new problem it cannot solve. The edge is the impasse of continuing not to solve it. The vortex is constraints loosening and chunks coming apart, changing the way of seeing. The bundle is the solution being carried out quickly and without hesitation.
  • Aesthetics (art): The field is one judgment. The wave is the imagination and the ideas of reason. The edge is the sublime, where the imagination falls short of the ideas of reason and the vital forces are momentarily checked. Vortex and bundle are not separated: they at once flow out all the more strongly.
  • Management studies (team transitions): The field is a project team (days to months). The wave is the framework set at the first meeting, and deadlines and outside demands. The edge is dropping old patterns and re-engaging with the outside at the midpoint toward the deadline. Vortex and bundle are not separated: the team enters a second phase with an agreed plan.

Quantum theory. Zurek (2003) writes of decoherence and einselection as two views of the same process. A quantum system interacts with its environment, and only the few states that can be copied many times over into the environment remain. This is theory 9 in the physics record.

Psychology (insight). Knoblich et al. (1999) write that in problems that need insight, an initial representation from past experience hides the solution and an impasse arises. As processes that change the representation they name constraint relaxation and the decomposition of familiar chunks, and test them with matchstick arithmetic problems. This is theory 1 in the psychology record.

Aesthetics. Kant's Critique of Judgment (1790) writes that in the beautiful, the imagination and the understanding agree in free play, and in the sublime, the imagination falls short of the ideas of reason and the vital forces are momentarily checked, then at once flow out all the more strongly. Theories in aesthetics are not theories that have verified phenomena, but analyses of aesthetic judgment. This is theory 3 in the aesthetics record.

Management studies (team transitions). Gersick (1988) observed eight teams over their whole life and writes that teams proceeded within the framework set at the first meeting, dropped their old ways and re-engaged with the outside at the calendar midpoint toward the deadline, and entered a second phase with a new plan. It is also written that in one team whose opinions remained split even at the midpoint, the leader dissolved the group and decided the proposal alone. This is theory 4 in the management studies record.

Everyday phenomena and research analyses. Quantum theory and phase transitions: the phenomenon is that when water cools into ice, crystals form separately here and there, and mismatches remain at the borders. The theory of Zurek (1996) and others, and experiments using crystals of 16 ions (Ulm et al. 2013, Pyka et al. 2013), write that in a phase transition the mismatches between orientations chosen independently in each region remain as defects, and the number of defects is set by the speed at which the transition is crossed. Psychology: the phenomenon is that when asked to do a task you do not want to do and taking it on for a small reward, you tell yourself "it was interesting" (cognitive dissonance). Diamond (2013) writes that when switching ways of seeing, one suppresses the previous set and loads another way of seeing into working memory, and the cost decreases with preparation but does not vanish. Aesthetics: the phenomena are fear and being drawn in arising at the same time before a fierce storm or a high mountain (the sublime), and, in Noh, becoming the flower because the viewer does not know (Zeami's "what is hidden is the flower"). Okakura Kakuzō (1906) writes of putting weight on the process of seeking perfection rather than on perfection, and that only those who complete the imperfect in their minds find beauty. Management studies: the phenomenon is a group suddenly changing how it works in the middle of a school term. Gersick (1988) writes that eight teams dropped their old ways and made a transition at the midpoint toward the deadline.

Myth is also included in art. The first volume of the Kojiki tells the story of Izanagi and Izanami circling the heavenly pillar. The two deities descend to the island of Onogoro and set up the heavenly pillar and a great hall; Izanami speaks of a place on Izanami's body "that has not grown together", and Izanagi of a place "that has grown in excess". Izanagi says "吾與汝行廻逢是天之御柱而、爲美斗能麻具波比" (you and I shall go around this heavenly pillar, meet, and unite), and Izanami goes around from the right and Izanagi from the left, and they meet. Here Izanami speaks first, "阿那邇夜志愛袁登古袁" (oh, what a fine young man), and then Izanagi says "阿那邇夜志愛袁登賣袁" (oh, what a fine young woman). After they have spoken, Izanagi says "女人先言不良" (it is not good that the woman spoke first), but the two unite, and the child born is Hiruko, who is put in a boat of reeds and set adrift. The two go up to the heavenly deities, who divine and say "因女先言而不良" (it was not good because the woman spoke first). The two go down and circle the pillar again, and this time Izanagi speaks first; the birth of the land continues from the island Awaji-no-ho-no-sawake, and the Land of the Eight Great Islands is born. (The English glosses in parentheses are Claude's.)

Fitted to the five stages, the field is the island of Onogoro and its pillar; the wave is the difference between the place "that has not grown together" and the place "that has grown in excess"; the edge is where they circle the pillar from right and left and meet; the vortex is exchanging words and a child being born (Hiruko is set adrift, a form that falls short); and the bundle is the birth of the land that follows after circling again. This matching is Claude's reading; the Kojiki does not say so. In Applying folk tales to understand spinor fields (2025, in Japanese), pjdhiro touches on this story, writing that it was "the first thing that came to mind with the order specified in qVq⁻¹" of spinors.

Yet the top page of "What Is Creation" does not take up what produces the waves until something swirls into a vortex — that is, the question of the driving source. That is what Love-Driven Development tries to see.

From classical physics to quantum theory

Physics, too, had a similar change of grip. At the end of the nineteenth century, the theories of mechanics, heat, light and electromagnetism had achieved great success, and some thought few problems remained. Following the small discrepancies that remained and peering inside the atom, everyday words such as position and speed did not work as they were. Even so, understanding that minute world became the explanation of the large world: why things are hard, why the sun keeps shining.

In 1900, Lord Kelvin, in a lecture, named "two clouds" hanging over the theories of heat and light. One was that no evidence could be found of the earth moving through the ether that was thought to carry light. The other was that the theory of equipartition of energy did not agree with the specific heats of gases and solids. In the same year, to explain the distribution of colors of light given off by hot bodies, Planck assumed that energy is exchanged in discrete lumps. The first cloud later became one of the problems that led to the theory of relativity, and the second, alongside Planck's work on thermal radiation, became one of the starting points of quantum theory.

In the world of quantum theory, the position and momentum of a particle cannot both be fixed as precisely as one likes at the same time (the uncertainty principle). Electrons behave both like grains and like waves. Intuitions built from everyday words are betrayed here again and again.

Even so, understanding this minute world explained the large world: atoms keeping their shape without collapsing (the uncertainty principle); things having volume and being hard even when many atoms gather (the Pauli exclusion principle); atoms binding into molecules (the chemical bond); hydrogen turning into helium at the center of the sun so that it keeps shining (the tunnel effect makes that reaction easier to occur). Semiconductors, and the computers we use now, are built on this understanding too.

Some research grasps anxiety from the side of the uncertainty of future threat and of the workings that prepare for it (Grupe and Nitschke 2013). Some research also holds that under uncertainty the stress response is not born after something happens, but is working from the start, and is held down only while one feels safe (Brosschot, Verkuil and Thayer 2018). In this reading, the signal of survival is not peculiar to some person or some stage of growth, but comes back to anyone once safety can no longer be felt.

Where does the feeling of safety come from? The same research counts, as one source of the feeling of safety, a strong network of connections for people and for animals that live in groups (Brosschot, Verkuil and Thayer 2018). From here, the relation of the two signals can be re-read in one form: writing the signal of survival as what comes back when safety cannot be felt, and the signal of trust as the feeling of safety that holds it down. "What Is Awareness" writes the two signals as two directions. Whether they are two separate signals, or one preparedness and what holds it down, touches the root of how "What Is Awareness" writes, so it has not been decided yet. Either way, the two are in the body at the same time and are not cut off from each other.

Grupe and Nitschke (2013) hold that uncertainty makes it difficult to prepare ahead and that this leads to anxiety, and describe moves to prepare for the uncertainty of future threat as adaptive while they are proportionate to the likelihood and severity of the threat.

Brosschot, Verkuil and Thayer (2018) placed the stress response as a default that is always on and is held down by the prefrontal cortex only when safety is felt. When safety can no longer be felt, that holding down comes off at once. They also write that the holding down coming off does not cause the stress response but allows it. As things that damage the feeling of safety, they name loneliness, low social status, urban environments and adversity in early childhood, and count a strong network of connections as one source of safety.

There is also a foothold for the reading that if not seeing ahead is held as a state known to be unseen, alarms are less likely to sound. Yu and Dayan (2005) separate the uncertainty of knowing that cues are unreliable within a familiar situation (expected uncertainty) from the uncertainty caused by an unannounced switch of situation (unexpected uncertainty), and propose assigning them to separate systems of neural modulation. In people with more than ten thousand hours of meditation, activity in the insula and amygdala was lower before pain (in the anticipation phase), while during pain activity in the insula and cingulate was, if anything, stronger; pain intensity was reported the same as by novices, and unpleasantness as less (Lutz et al. 2013). It is not that the signal stops sounding; it does not sound in the anticipation phase, and sounds when the event comes.

Where do the feelings of trust and love come from?

People come to trust someone, or to like someone. Is that feeling born each time from looking at the person in front of them? pjdhiro's words in the previous chapter named, as sources bearing on the definitions of trust and love, Freud's transference, Klein's object relations and Jung's archetypes. Of the psychology that influenced pjdhiro, pjdhiro names Freud's transference first.

pjdhiro's impression (translated by Claude):The phenomenon of transference became a starting point for me to think, from experience, about emotional contagion in general.

Psychoanalysis has written that some of the feelings of trust and love come not only from the person in front of one, but also from patterns already within oneself. In 1912 Freud wrote that, from what they are born with and the influences of childhood, people acquire a pattern of under what conditions and how they love, and repeat it throughout life. Part of the pattern remains unknown to the person. When the need for love is not fully satisfied in reality, that pattern is turned toward new people one meets. Freud called its being turned toward the doctor in the setting of treatment transference. In the same paper Freud writes that emotional ties such as sympathy, friendship and trust, however pure they feel to the person, lead back, when traced, to sexual drives.

In 1946 Klein wrote that the infant receives its first object split into a good one that satisfies and a bad one that does not, and that this becomes the dividing line between love and hate. In 1917 Jung wrote that within transference, primordial images common to people, which cannot be explained by the person's memories, are also projected (the chapter "What is the unconscious?"). In the same paper Jung writes that when such images are projected onto people around one, they become excessive exaltation or contempt, producing infatuation and conflict. In a 1919 paper Jung calls inherited patterns of the mind by the word archetypes.

What the three have in common is the view that some of the feelings of trust and love come not only from the person in front of one, but from patterns one is not conscious of. The patterns the three describe differ: a version of childhood relationships (Freud), images split into good and bad (Klein), images common to people (Jung). Rather than deciding which is right, one can also see them, like the blind men and the elephant, as touching different aspects of the same thing.

What looks threatening changes with the stage of development

Research on adult development writes that the mechanism by which people make meaning of themselves and the world is reorganized several times over a lifetime, and has called each of these a stage (see also: What are stages of mental development?, in Japanese). It is written that the later the stage, the less need there is for defensive moves, but also that maladaptation and pathology can occur at any period.

How do reassurance and dependence differ from trust?

Idealization was a movement to protect oneself from anxiety. Then is the feeling of being protected, of relying on someone with reassurance, the same as trust? pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):For me, this year of collaborating with AI was exactly a sense of reassurance between me and a boundless AI, the brain of the world. Connected by an umbilical cord, entrusting my arguments with reassurance.

Winnicott wrote that the chief characteristic of infancy is dependence (1960). Wherever there is an infant there is always someone caring for it, and at first infant and care are one unit that cannot be separated. Winnicott called not only physically holding the infant but the whole of the provision surrounding the infant "holding", and wrote that it is reliable, not mechanically, but in a way that includes empathy for the other. Dependence is at first a dependence in which the infant is not even aware of the care, and later becomes a dependence aware of needing care. From there, Winnicott writes, as memories of care accumulate and a feeling of being able to rely on the environment (confidence) grows, the infant moves in the direction of managing without actual care.

In Winnicott's way of writing, relying and being held is the foothold for the feeling of being able to rely. Dependence can be read as not written as something discarded before reaching trust. Reassurance and dependence and trust can be written as two, but may not be separable. Whether trust always comes from accumulation is not decided on this page.

On the other hand, the same word "reassurance" is also sought from a different need. Maslow wrote of the need for safety as a need for a predictable, orderly, reliable world (1943). It includes preferring the familiar and the known, and looking for someone to protect one or a strong person to rely on. When, in the psychological safety section of the part on development, pjdhiro saw reassurance and safety sought in a spirit of self-protection or opportunism as a state like dependence through psychological defense mechanisms (the fold in the chapter "Where are the words of business misunderstood?"), it was reassurance of this side. Both kinds of reassurance are expressions of people always relying on something somewhere, and cannot be sorted into good and bad. Set side by side, the two kinds of reassurance look different in what they seek protection from.

More — can the stability of being connected without confirming be measured?

In the hand-holding experiment, the person whose hand was held was visible and being touched. Then has the stability of being connected to someone unseen been measured? In part, it has.

Attachment research has seen people as holding, in their minds, images of others they can rely on. When they feel threatened, people reach, below the level of consciousness, for the image of someone not in front of them. Even just calling that image to mind brings reassurance, at least for a time. That even with someone unseen, an image in the mind brings reassurance has been shown by experiments up to this point.

However, what has been measured is the effect of images of others who can be named. There are also experiments that measured something close to an other given no name. When people were asked to record, moment by moment, how strongly they felt an invisible presence while watching footage of magnificent nature, the sense lasted from beginning to end, and afterward it was felt not as an agent like a god or Buddha, but as something like the air enveloping nature.

pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):I express this by applying the word Fūjin, the wind god.

The other is an experiment that exposed people, without their noticing, to words suggesting the sacred. In a task of rearranging sentences, words such as spirit, divine and sacred were included in a form that would not be noticed. Then the brain-wave response was measured when people made errors on a subsequent task. This response is held to be linked to bracing against one's own errors. In people who believe in God, this response became smaller. In people who do not, it became larger instead. The authors conclude that for believers, religion may soften anxious responses to one's own errors.

In both experiments the other was not visible and was given no name. However, the nature-footage experiment produced the sense by showing a grand landscape, and did not measure stability itself. The effect in the sacred-words experiment occurs only within a faith the person already holds. pjdhiro's image of the top of the head being connected to something by something like an umbilical cord (the fold in the chapter "Isn't Love like gravity?") is a stability of connection with an other who cannot be named, welling up where there is neither landscape nor teaching. Mikulincer, Gillath and Shaver (2002) showed that presenting words representing threat too fast to reach consciousness makes mental images (representations) of attachment figures more accessible. This effect occurred only for attachment figures, and replicated across changes of task and experiment. Shaver and Mikulincer (2008) summarize that having people call to mind supportive attachment figures improves mood, compassion and tolerance toward outgroups, and that this also occurs in insecurely attached people. That is, even just evoking, below consciousness, the image of an attachment figure being there may bring reassurance, at least for a time.

More — how certain are intention, control and consciousness?

The word "business development" suggests the act of intentionally starting a business and moving it along as one wishes. Let us look at the three in turn from the side of research.

  • Intention: Mintzberg and Waters (1983) distinguished strategies that were intended and realized, strategies that were intended but not realized, and strategies that took shape without being intended (emergent strategies). Mintzberg also wrote in 1994 that every strategy has some emergent quality.
  • Control: Knight (1921) distinguished "risk", which can be estimated by probability, from "uncertainty", where even the probability is unknown. And Knight thought that business profit arises from taking on this uncertainty.
  • Consciousness: Weick et al. (1999) wrote that people, after starting to act, use the results as clues to find out, looking back, what is happening. pjdhiro sees this as similar to "dots connect looking backward" in Jobs's speech. Wegner (1999, 2003) argued that the very feeling that "I did it" may be an interpretation the mind constructs by inferring from the relation between thought and action.

Lined up like this, "business development" may have the aspect of being a name for what happened, arranged afterward into a single story. At the same time, in Knight's distinction, business is also carried forward while taking on a future whose probabilities are unknown. This page asks about what did not make it into that story, and what was moving before the story. The same shape appears within one person, in a group of people, and in society.

More — intended strategy, and strategy realized without being intended

The view that looks at both what is moved along intentionally and what takes shape without intention also exists in strategy research. In a 1983 chapter, Mintzberg and Waters gave four definitions of strategy. Two of them are strategy as a plan set out in advance, and strategy as a pattern seen in a stream of an organization's decisions and actions. Strategy as pattern, they write, is a definition concerned not with intention but with what was realized, that is, with what actually happened in the organization.

Combining these two definitions, they drew three strategies in one diagram: strategy intended and realized as intended; strategy intended but not realized; and strategy realized as a pattern without being particularly intended (emergent strategy).

What they followed were the strategies of organizations such as companies, newspapers, universities and government agencies. The range pjdhiro points to with the word development is not limited to organizations; it extends to change within one person, to families, and to the movements of society.

More — how do conflict, cognitive dissonance and the double bind differ?

All three look like two things in opposition. What differs is where, and what collides with what.

  • Conflict: two or more motives or goals competing within one person. Lewin divided this into three types: wanting both (approach–approach), wanting to avoid both (avoidance–avoidance), and being both drawn to and wanting to avoid one goal (approach–avoidance). Discrepancies of wishes, opinions or norms between people are also called interpersonal conflict (Saishin shinrigaku jiten).
  • Cognitive dissonance: a state within one person in which things one knows or believes do not fit with one another. The misfit produces pressure to reduce it, and people may reduce it by changing their own thoughts or attitudes (Dejitaru Daijisen; Festinger and Carlsmith 1959). In Festinger and Carlsmith's experiment, people made to tell others that a boring task had been interesting, and paid one dollar, later themselves rated the task as interesting. Those paid twenty dollars did not change their ratings. It is written that they persuaded themselves to fit what they had said.

More — (2) biases decrease (the error of mistaking the abstract for the concrete, and ways to reduce it)

One is less often misled by things easy to perceive, like vortices. pjdhiro sees this as a way of dealing with the distortions and limits of cognition. In chapter 3 of Science and the Modern World (1925), the philosopher Whitehead named the error of mistaking the abstract for the concrete "the fallacy of misplaced concreteness". In the same passage Whitehead also wrote that the intellect need not fall into this trap. The direction is not to discard abstractions but to use them knowing they are abstractions. The difference between giving a vortex a name and treating a named vortex as a thing has the same shape as this distinction.

In psychology, ways of reducing bias have been studied. In 2015 Morewedge and colleagues reported that a single training session, with a game or a video, reduced six biases, including confirmation bias and anchoring, by medium to large amounts, and that the effect remained two months later.

The same thing has been said in investment practice. In 2000 Barber and Odean examined the trades of more than 66,000 households and showed that the households that traded most earned an annual return of 11.4%, well below the market's 17.9%, and that this can be explained by overconfidence. In a 1994 talk, the investor Charlie Munger cited the saying "to a man with only a hammer, every problem looks like a nail", and recommended holding the ways of thinking of many fields combined like a latticework. It is an example of speaking of increasing viewpoints and reducing bias as one measure.

More — the four explorations and the shapes of physics

The driving source and gravity. In the figure of the gravity well, the things around revolve along the hollow made by the mass at the center.

Kesson and the phase of waves. In quantum mechanics, the state of a particle is written as a complex-valued wave (the wave function). The probability of finding the particle at a place is set by the square of the absolute value of that wave, and the direction of the wave (its phase) does not enter. Shifting the phase of the whole wave uniformly changes nothing in the observed results. But when two waves overlap, the difference in phase shows up clearly as interference fringes. The phase itself does not show up in numbers on its own, yet where waves overlap it produces a definite difference.

This is where it overlaps with the shape of kesson-driven thinking: a value that is 0 in cognition and yet works. What overlaps is the behavior. A value on the imaginary axis is a magnitude whose projection onto the axis of action is 0. Phase is a direction that disappears when the absolute value is taken. The reasons they do not show up in numbers differ, but they share not showing up in numbers on their own and yet working.

Awareness and observation. In the quantum world, measuring can change the state of what is measured. For example, measuring position disturbs momentum (the error in measuring position and the disturbance given to momentum cannot both be made zero, but it is known that their product can be made smaller than the limit once thought). In models of measurement, during measurement the object being measured and a small part of the apparatus (the probe) are taken together as one system, and its change over time is written. After the measurement, only the probe side is measured and read off on the scale. The model writes only this far; it does not write that the observing side and the observed side become inseparable. This page sees this form, writing the two together during measurement, as an analogy overlapping with where metacognition, dividing oneself, ends up.

Creation and the fluctuations of the field. In quantum field theory, particles appear as one of the ways a field vibrates (an excitation). Separately, as a matter of fluids, in superfluid helium the flow around a vortex (circulation) appears only in fixed units. In fluids, there are examples where the vortex itself appears as a form that cannot be cut off from the fluid.

Taking the estimate literally — do quanta work in the nerves?

The human body, too, is made of matter, and it is hard to think of it as outside the laws of physics. However, how far quantum effects work in the processing of neural signals has not yet been answered even among researchers.

Quantum effects working inside living things are studied as quantum biology. For example, it is known that the tunnel effect, in which particles slip through barriers, is involved in enzyme reactions.

For the brain, there are several proposals. Penrose and Hameroff proposed that quantum processes proceed in the microtubules inside neurons, and that each time they spontaneously collapse under certain conditions, a moment of consciousness arises. Fisher proposed that the nuclear spins of phosphorus atoms may be able to hold quantum information for a long time in the brain. There are also proposals and calculations that quantum coherence inside ion channels may be involved in how ions pass through.

There are also calculations against. Tegmark estimated that in the brain at body temperature, quantum superpositions break down in an extremely short time, falling far short of the speed of neural signals. Current neuroscience writes the release and reception of neurotransmitters, and the electrical signals that follow, almost entirely in classical physics. Where things stand now is that whether quantum effects work in the processing of neural signals has been neither confirmed nor fully ruled out.
