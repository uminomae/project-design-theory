More — what a stage is

Some theories hold that ego development continues after we become adults. Cook-Greuter writes that on the path of adulthood, too, people may expand their awareness, stay where they are, or contract (p.2). She also writes that what changes from stage to stage is not the challenges people meet, but how they relate to them (p.7). What looks most threatening also differs by stage (pp.9–10).

The stage names in the table are Cook-Greuter's. People are said to move through the stages in order, from the top of the table downward, and the later stages can only be reached by passing through the earlier ones (p.2).

Cook-Greuter writes that, so that the stage names are not read as a ranking in which "higher is better," she speaks of "earlier" and "later" rather than "lower" and "higher" (p.7). She also writes that the stage descriptions are idealized, and are not to be seen as a simple staircase (p.4).

People are not fixed at one stage either. Nobody is 100% at one stage, and a person's responses are said to span at least three stages (p.5). People may also fall back to earlier stages under stress, or because of life circumstances and environment (pp.5, 7).

Stages are written about in more detail in What Are Stages of Mental Development (in Japanese).

Key points

Definition The term "psychological safety" has been used in therapy (Rogers, 1954), in training where people learn in groups (Schein and Bennis, 1965), and in organizational change (Schein, 1995). Schein wrote of it as the sense that one can learn something new without losing one's identity or sense of competence, and put it as the first condition for lowering the anxiety of learning. In 1999 Edmondson defined it as "a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking." The history is in the Part 2 chapter on the history of the term; the content of the definition (not being embarrassed, rejected or punished) is in the chapter on shame anxiety
This page's viewpoint In pjdhiro's view, a state without psychological safety is a state of defense against anxiety, and shame anxiety is one of the anxieties within it. What is called high psychological safety is a state of mature defense. What becomes the main fear, and how people protect themselves, differ by stage of development (Cook-Greuter, 2013)
Common misunderstandings That it is about physical safety. That it is a system of not being punished. That it means getting along, or having no rigor. That it is about one's own reassurance and safety. That it can be created by declaring "Please say anything." That higher is better in every organization
Conditions where it matters It carries more weight in work that involves uncertainty and calls for creativity or collaboration (Edmondson and Lei, 2014). Speaking up to stop and report errors and dangers seems, judging from the example of aviation, to have been treated as needed regardless of the nature of the work. pjdhiro thinks it may work in work that deals with intangible things, such as services and design

Stages of psychological safety

The best-known stages of psychological safety are the four stages put out by Clark and his company, LeaderFactor: inclusion safety, learner safety, contributor safety and challenger safety. People and groups are said to move through them in this order. The same company's technical report states that the answers to the 12 survey items came together as a single factor. In the answers, the four stages appeared as shades of a single dimension. The report has no data that followed the stages progressing in order over time. Nor did we find any peer-reviewed paper that tested these four stages.

Some research that followed teams over time does not fit the shape of climbing a staircase. In a study that measured 61 self-managed student teams (236 people) working on a five-month research project three times, at the beginning, middle and end, psychological safety fell from the beginning to the end.

pjdhiro overlaps this decline with what they have seen in close relationships.

pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):From my experience too, in close relationships, such as between spouses or friends, I often see mental growth like positive transference and negative transference. It isn't that expectations and ideals head in a straight line toward reassurance, wrapped in happiness. Defenses like idealization peel away, anxiety arises, and from there, by accepting reality, I think there is a psychological safety like resignation.

The authors of this study explain the decline by the stages of the work. At the beginning, many questions are allowed in order to grasp the task together. As the task comes into view, questions and comments are more easily taken as things that slow the work down. As the deadline nears, the team closes, and it becomes harder to reopen discussion. That is their explanation. They do not explain it as a process in which idealization peels away. Because the study measured three times over five months, whether safety comes back after falling was not followed.

More — how what comes after idealization peels away has been written about

In research that followed spouses from the time they were newly married, satisfaction also starts high and falls, on average. A study that followed 464 newlyweds for four years called this decline one of the most robust findings in research on marriage, and also showed that there are groups whose satisfaction does not fall (Lavner and Bradbury, 2010). In a study that followed 222 couples, married about half a year, for three years, satisfaction clearly fell overall, but those who strongly saw their partner as matching their own ideals did not show a fall in satisfaction (Murray et al., 2011). Idealization in that study is the degree to which people see their partner as matching an ideal image; it was not measured as a defense. In research on couples, the fading of idealization and the fall in satisfaction (disillusionment) have been studied as a forerunner of breaking up (Niehuis et al., 2013).

The psychoanalyst Klein wrote about the process of mourning in a form close to pjdhiro's view (1940). Idealization is a defense bound up with denial, and at the same time an intermediate step essential to the development of the mind. When hatred of the lost person arises, trust in the good object is shaken and idealization is painfully disturbed. Klein writes that as people gradually regain trust in external objects, they can bear to realize that the person was not perfect, and yet not lose trust and love. She also writes that painful experiences of every kind have something in common with mourning, and that meeting and overcoming adversity involves mental work similar to mourning. Klein's account is inferred from the clinical work with infants and with mourning; it does not measure couples or teams. Idealization itself is looked at in the Love-Driven Development chapter "How do idealization and trust differ?" (in Japanese).

pjdhiro's view places the disillusionment that research on couples has studied as a forerunner of breaking up on a path toward a psychological safety like resignation. We leave the two side by side, without fitting one to the other.

The four left-hand columns are what Cook-Greuter wrote for each stage. The right-hand column is not written in the research. Claude arranged it from the left-hand columns.

At the Conformist stage, rules are something to follow without question, and things are divided into right and wrong. At the Expert stage, one's own way looks like the only right way. Whichever of these stages the people pushing a measure are at, the measure can be read as tending to take the shape of a single "right answer." At the Pluralist stage the direction reverses: because every view is treated as equal, deciding tends to stall, Cook-Greuter writes.

On this page we have not come across research that directly examined the relation between the stage of those who push measures and the shape of the measures and rules. A close piece of research is the study by Rooke and Torbert. The two took part in the development efforts of ten organizations, about four years per organization, measured the CEOs' stage of development with a test, and set this beside where the organizations went. Torbert's stage names correspond one-to-one with the stages in the table above (Opportunist is Self-protective, Diplomat is Conformist, and Expert, Achiever and Strategist have the same names).

All five organizations with a CEO at the Strategist stage changed for the better. Of the five organizations with a CEO at an earlier stage, three fell into crisis. The other two changed for the better, and in both the CEO had a consultant or executive at the Strategist stage as a close adviser.

The authors also describe, stage by stage, how people face negative feedback. The Opportunist rejects feedback, and the Diplomat avoids conflict to protect their own and the other's face. The Expert accepts feedback only from masters in the same field, and the Achiever welcomes feedback that bears on goals. They also write that a Diplomat CEO would feel the threat of losing face in speaking of their own weaknesses in public.

What this study looked at was not the shape of measures and rules, but whether the organizations changed. It is also a retrospective look at ten organizations in which the authors themselves took part as consultants.

Part 2

Seeing it as defense against anxiety

History of the term

Part 2 begins by looking, in order of years, at where the term psychological safety came from. In this history, Schein writes of the forces that stop change as psychological defenses (the 1995 section).

Of the literature traced in this chapter, Schein's 1995 paper and Edmondson's 1999 paper were checked in the text. Lewin's 1947 paper was checked in a copy placed on the Internet Archive, and Rogers's 1954 essay in a copy, placed by a third party, of the full text reprinted in a 1970 collection. Schein and Bennis's book was checked only as far as the fragments found by a full-text search of the book.

1947: Lewin's three steps of change

The social psychologist Lewin described group change in three steps: unfreezing (if necessary) the present level, moving to a new level, and freezing at that level. Unfreezing is written about as a matter of the forces that loosen custom, overcoming the inner resistance of habit. The paper has no words that discuss safety of the mind or anxiety.

1954: Rogers's conditions for creativity

The term "psychological safety" was used by Rogers, of client-centered therapy, in his 1954 essay on creativity, as a condition under which creativity grows. It is said to be established by accepting the individual as of unconditional worth, by an absence of external evaluation, and by understanding empathically. Creativity here includes external products such as works, theories and inventions, and creating one's own personality anew in psychotherapy is counted as part of the same process. Rogers's interest in creativity came from that experience of psychotherapy.

1965: Unfreezing in Schein and Bennis

Schein and Bennis's book deals with training in which people learn in groups (laboratory training). Psychological safety is placed as one part of Lewin's unfreezing step, and is said to be created by reducing threat or by removing barriers to change. The book lays out several theories that explain learning in training. One is the interpersonal theory of Sullivan and Rogers; another is psychoanalysis, which deals with unconscious motives. But nothing in the fragments we could read shows that the term "psychological safety" was borrowed from Rogers. The chapter in the book by Bugental and Tannenbaum cites Maslow's distinction between deficiency motivation and growth motivation, and places sensitivity training on the side of growth.

pjdhiro reads these two ways of creating it from the side of psychological defense.

pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):Reducing threat is a state where psychological defense mechanisms aren't triggered; barriers to change means giving up psychological defense mechanisms and accepting reality. I think this has been reported a lot in counseling practice, by Shinya Nitta, Rogers and others. The methods and the difficulties.

The methods and difficulties, on the counseling side, of the process in which defenses loosen and reality is accepted are looked at in the chapter "Going back to defense, or keeping on facing it" of What Is Anxiety (in Japanese).

1995: Schein looks back on Lewin

In 1995, in a paper looking back on Lewin's theory, Schein writes that he built his own theory of change on the foundation of Lewin's "unfreezing, changing, refreezing" model. These three words are Schein's phrasing; in Lewin's original they were unfreezing, moving and freezing. When Schein studied the attitude change of prisoners of war in the Korean War, he says, this model became the foundation for explaining the deep changes that had occurred in the prisoners. According to Schein, many of the forces that stop change are individual psychological defenses or group norms. He writes that he came to understand the whole of this only after turning to cognitive psychology's studies of perceptual defense, to psychoanalysis and the Tavistock work dealing with denial, splitting and projection, and to Argyris's research on defensive routines. Schein unpacked the unfreezing step into three processes and placed psychological safety as one of them. The three processes are receiving disconfirming information, the arousal of survival anxiety or guilt, and the creation of psychological safety. Without enough psychological safety, Schein writes, the information is denied or defended against in other ways, and no change occurs. The denial, splitting and projection that Schein lists are what psychoanalysis has called defense mechanisms, and they also appear in the table on the page What Are Psychological Defense Mechanisms (in Japanese). The pair of learning anxiety and psychological safety is looked at later, in the chapter How has the literature on psychological safety written about shame anxiety?, through a paper on organizational culture from the same year.

pjdhiro sees this line of thought as also answering a question Shinya Nitta raised: "Are expectations bad?" Schein had written that information that disconfirms expectations becomes an entrance to change when there is enough psychological safety, and turns toward denial and defense when there is not. Nitta's question, and how people act after their expectations are disappointed, are looked at in the chapter "How do expectation and trust differ?" of What I Want to Call Love-Driven Development (in Japanese).

1999: Edmondson's definition for teams

Edmondson defined team psychological safety and named Schein and Bennis's book as its root. Where she writes that admitting errors or asking for help carries the risk of losing face, she cites the sociologist Goffman's research on face. She does not cite Rogers.

What the history tells us

Names from developmental psychology such as Erikson or Piaget do not appear in the literature traced here. Maslow's safety needs are not cited either.

To sum up what the history tells us: the term "psychological safety" has been used in different places with different contents, in therapy, in training where people learn in groups, in organizational change, and in research on teams. Between Rogers and Schein and Bennis, nothing has been found that shows the term was inherited. After 1999 too, the wording of the definition and the level at which it is measured keep moving (the section That you are safe as long as you are not punished). It is a term that spread without inheriting a single definition. Every author was writing on top of the experience of therapy, or of Lewin's theory and the research on prisoners.

Set beside this page's two viewpoints, it can be read as follows (Claude's reading). The viewpoint of defense against anxiety overlaps with the language of defense that Schein took in from psychoanalysis. For the viewpoint of stages of development, no direct source can be seen in the history; it is a view that came from outside the history.

How has the literature on psychological safety written about shame anxiety?

In pjdhiro's view, one of the anxieties that defenses try to protect against is shame anxiety. This chapter looks at how the literature on psychological safety has written about the anxiety of being shamed, in the order of Schein's two anxieties and Edmondson's definition for teams. At the end, we look at Brown, who spoke about shame and vulnerability from outside the literature on psychological safety.

Schein's (1995) two anxieties

In a 1995 working paper on organizational culture, Schein paired two anxieties: the anxiety about learning something new (learning anxiety) and the anxiety that one will not survive without changing (survival anxiety). Psychological safety is placed as the first condition for lowering learning anxiety: feeling that one can learn something new without losing one's sense of who one is or one's sense of competence. He also writes that when learning anxiety stays high, people become defensive, deny reality, rationalize, and blame others. Schein's view is that survival anxiety is usually already high enough, because people are told daily that their old habits no longer fit present reality, and that it is learning anxiety that should be lowered.

Edmondson's (1999) definition for teams

It was Edmondson's 1999 paper that set psychological safety as a research term. It is defined as "a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking."

The paper places the root of the idea in Schein and Bennis's 1965 book. Edmondson summarizes it as a book that argued for the need to create psychological safety so that people feel secure and capable of changing. Where the term came from was looked at in the chapter History of the term at the start of Part 2.

The confidence that the team will not embarrass someone for speaking up is part of the content of the 1999 definition. The paper writes that admitting errors or asking for help carries the risk of being seen as incompetent and losing face, so people tend to hold back from such behavior.

Alongside this, the same confidence includes not being rejected and not being punished. The paper has no word referring to physical safety. What it writes of as protected is protection from embarrassment, rejection and punishment, and being oneself, a sense of competence, and self-esteem.

The confidence that one will not be embarrassed, rejected or punished is said to stem from mutual respect and trust. Psychological safety is also described as something that "involves but goes beyond interpersonal trust" and as a climate in which "people are comfortable being themselves." In the paper's discussion it is restated as a climate mixing trust, respect for each other's competence, and caring about each other as people. Edmondson's definition wraps not being punished, and being able to be oneself in respect and trust, within a single paragraph.

The definition is not an individual's feeling but a belief shared by the team. The paper also writes that this belief is usually tacit, "taken for granted."

The paper sets up and tests the hypothesis that team psychological safety promotes learning behavior. The examples of learning behavior it gives are seeking feedback, sharing information, asking for help, talking about errors, and experimenting.

How this confidence tends to be received in a shrunken form in the workplace is looked at in Common misunderstandings in Part 4.

Brown's (2010) shame and vulnerability

One person who has spoken about shame from outside the literature on psychological safety is Brown, a qualitative researcher with a degree in social work. What we cite here is a 2010 talk in which she spoke about her own research, not a research paper.

In the talk, Brown says shame is easily understood as the fear of disconnection: the fear that if people know or see some part of us, we will not be worthy of connection. In Edmondson's paper, the risk of embarrassment was written of as the risk of being seen as incompetent and losing face. Brown's way of putting it places the weight on falling out of relationships.

Brown says that vulnerability lies at the core of shame and fear and of the struggle over whether we are worthy. At the same time, she says, it also seems to be where joy, creativity, belonging and love are born. What the people with a strong sense of worthiness had in common was that they embraced vulnerability, and she says that we usually numb vulnerability. This move of not feeling vulnerability can also be read as one of the defenses (Claude's reading).

Defenses differ in maturity

The view of pjdhiro quoted in the overview sees a state of mature defense as what we call high psychological safety. This chapter looks at what can be said about the maturity of defenses from this page's sources: the arrangement of the kinds and levels of defense; how immature defenses relate to personality disorders and workplace bullying; and how much defense is needed, in that order.

As we saw in the chapter The mind has ways of protecting itself, Vaillant distinguished "mature" defenses such as sublimation and humor from "immature" defenses such as projection, and held that which one uses makes a great difference to mental health. He also writes that when anxiety and loneliness are eased by empathy, "immature" defenses can come undone.

In the table of stages of development in Part 1, the later the stage, the more mature the defenses listed. In the Self-protective row are fantasy, acting out and projection; in the Strategist row, suppression, positive reframing, altruism and non-hostile humor; in the Construct-aware and later row, sublimation, non-hostile humor and bracketing. Cook-Greuter also writes that the later the stage, the less need there is for defensive moves.

Kinds and levels of defense

The table arranging the kinds and levels of defense is on the page What Are Psychological Defense Mechanisms (in Japanese). Perry's Defense Mechanism Rating Scales arrange 30 defenses into seven levels and group them into three categories: immature, neurotic and mature. Each level is described by what it protects the mind from. The level that protects against the imminent threat of being punished or abandoned, and the level that protects self-esteem from the feelings of shame or helplessness produced by failure or criticism, are in the immature category. The most mature level is described as taking in stress without distortion, acknowledging one's own limits, and relying on help when it is available.

Immature defenses, personality disorders and workplace bullying

Vaillant writes that immature defenses are common in personality disorders, and produce behavior that looks irritating to those around. There is also research that depicts workplace bullying as defenses such as splitting and projection operating among the people who bully, the people who are bullied, and the organization. The figures and limits of that research, and a view that sets the severity and type of personality side by side, are looked at on the page What Are Psychological Defense Mechanisms (in Japanese).

A paper reviewing research on the perpetration of bullying writes, citing earlier studies, that when a workplace's psychosocial safety climate (a shared perception that management prioritizes workers' psychological health) is strong, opportunities for aggression are held down. Psychosocial safety climate is defined as a concept separate from Edmondson's psychological safety (the belief that it is safe to take interpersonal risks). On the relation between the two, pjdhiro sees it as follows.

pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):I think they're pointing at much the same thing. Because the individual and the organization interact.

The relation between a person's defenses and the strength of the threat in the setting is looked at later, in the section How much defense is needed.

How much defense is needed

If defenses differ in maturity, how much defense is needed should differ too. From the name of the term itself, pjdhiro raises the question as follows.

pjdhiro's question (translated by Claude):Going by the term psychological safety itself, you can infer both defending for the sake of safety, and being in a state that is safe even without defending, can't you?

In Perry's rating scales, the most mature level is described as taking in internal and external stress without distortion, acknowledging one's own limits, and relying on help when it is available. The more immature the level, the more stress is attributed to the outside as unbearable, parts of reality are not acknowledged, and images of oneself and others are distorted. The degrees of maturity are arranged as degrees of how far a threat can be taken in without distortion.

Even so, the mature level is also counted as a defense. Vaillant writes that even the most pathological defenses calm the mind, and sees defenses as coping responses, like fever. The sources we could read do not depict a state without defense. Brought close to the sources' language, "a state that is safe even without defending" can be read as close to the mature level at which a threat can be taken in without distortion.

Which level of defense appears also depends on the strength of the threat. Vaillant writes that when empathy eases anxiety and loneliness, immature defenses fall apart. Perry's table also counts confiding in and relying on others as a mature defense. In research on workplace bullying, supervisors were more likely to abuse subordinates when their own identity was threatened, and job insecurity also intensified aggression. Schein writes that balancing the amount of threat produced by disconfirming information with enough psychological safety to take it in is the key to moving change forward (the 1995 section of the history of the term). In this argument, psychological safety is placed as what lets the information be taken in without defending against it.

This page reads the degree to which defense is needed as changing with both the strength of the threat in the setting and a person's ability to take in the threat without distortion. The view that a state of mature defense is what we call high psychological safety can be read as pointing to the mature end of this arrangement. We have not yet come across research that measured psychological safety and level of defense side by side in the same people or teams.

To understand the two viewpoints

Are the absence of threat and the presence of cues of safety the same?

Part 3 holds views that do not fit within the two viewpoints of defense against anxiety and stages of development, but are needed to understand them.

We set side by side sources that have written of the absence of threat and the presence of cues of safety or trust as separate things. Edmondson's definition wrapped protection from embarrassment, rejection and punishment together with respect and trust in a single confidence. There is a view that sees these two as separate axes.

pjdhiro's question (translated by Claude):I think there are two axes, something like polyvagal theory. Isn't Yamagishi saying something about reptiles?

Porges, of polyvagal theory, wrote in a 2022 paper that feeling safe is not the same as the removal of threat. He also writes that the usual way of thinking has assumed that removing threat makes us feel safe, and implicitly treated removing it as enough. When cues of safety are present, the social engagement system is said to be able to dampen responses to threat.

More — criticism of polyvagal theory

Polyvagal theory's account of the evolution of the nervous system is strongly disputed. A 2023 paper concluded that each of the theory's five premises is either untenable or unlikely. It also writes that reptiles, fish and birds have complex social behavior too, and that the claim that this is unique to mammals does not fit the evidence. Another paper says that the view of the brain as an onion, with new layers stacked on older ones, has long been rejected in neurobiology.

Porges replied in 2026. He accepted that birds, reptiles and fish also have social behavior, and responded that whether it rests on the same autonomic basis as in mammals is a separate matter. The dispute continues.

The criticisms we checked are aimed at the physiological and evolutionary account. They are not directly aimed at the distinction itself, that the removal of threat and feeling safe are different.

How do trust and psychological safety differ?

In 1999 Edmondson wrote that psychological safety "involves but goes beyond interpersonal trust." Then where do the two differ?

In a 2002 working paper, Edmondson writes of the difference from trust on three points: time frame, focus, and level of analysis. Psychological safety looks at the interpersonal consequences in the very near term. Trust is a matter of whether one gives others the benefit of the doubt. Psychological safety is a matter of whether others will give oneself the benefit of the doubt, and its focus is on oneself. A team member's words are quoted: "I don't have to wear a mask in this team…it's easy to be myself." Trust is said to concern mainly the relationship between two people, and psychological safety the group.

Edmondson and Lei's 2014 review describes psychological safety as "people's perceptions of the consequences of taking interpersonal risks in a particular context such as a workplace." It also arranges the research into three levels: measured as an individual's experience, measured as a group's climate, and measured as an organization's average.

The 1999 definition held that the confidence that one will not be embarrassed, rejected or punished stems from mutual respect and trust (How has the literature on psychological safety written about shame anxiety?). About the word trust that lies at that foundation, pjdhiro sees it as follows.

pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):If it's rapport or trust, those are difficult concepts, difficult enough that Love-Driven Development is searching for them, so I think plain, simple words like reassurance and safety lead to a lot of misunderstanding.

Why measures miss the mark

Common misunderstandings

This chapter looks at how psychological safety tends to be received in the workplace, and at the phenomena from which those readings arise.

Within research, too, the wording of the definition keeps moving. That higher is better and it is needed everywhere

Much research shows links with learning and performance, and it has been widely introduced. On the other hand, the 2014 review raises the possibility that, over time, people become too comfortable with each other and the emphasis drifts away from work and learning. It also writes that excessive psychological safety may lead people to spend time on things that do not matter, or to lose the motivation to learn.

Then is psychological safety needed in the same way in every kind of work and organization?

pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):In military-type organizations that treat individuals like parts, I think psychological safety is low, but that may work better. I think it's effective when dealing with intangible things like services and design (just a hunch).

The research side also has clues about the conditions under which it works. Edmondson's 1999 paper writes that, in very routine, repetitive work, learning behavior can lower efficiency and harm performance. At the same time, it goes on to say that even teams doing routine production need learning behavior for self-management and occasional process improvement. The 2014 review wrote that psychological safety carries more weight when there is uncertainty and creativity or collaboration is needed. The reason given is that without elements of uncertainty or collaboration, the need to face interpersonal risk is not prominent in the first place.

The review's angle is whether there is uncertainty and whether creativity or collaboration is needed; it does not cut by tangible versus intangible. Much work in services and design can be read as meeting this condition. Some work, such as research and development or surgery, deals with tangible things and still meets the condition, and some work, such as counter procedures, is intangible and routine. The two angles overlap a great deal, but are not the same.

Within the range we could check, we found no research that measured psychological safety and directly showed that performance was better when it was lower.

Then can the same be said of situations where errors or misconduct must be stopped?

pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):If you're talking in the context of compliance, misconduct or governance, that would be different, though.

The research so far has looked at speaking up directed toward learning and creativity. Speaking up in the direction of stopping and reporting errors and dangers seems, judging from the example of aviation, to have been treated as needed regardless of the nature of the work. Crew resource management training in aviation began by correcting the lack of assertiveness of junior crew members and the authoritarian behavior of captains, and later moved toward treating error as normal and managing it. Training in the leadership role that comes with command was also created for new captains.

More — psychological safety in the military

A 2024 paper by researchers at the U.S. Military Academy wrote that psychological safety that is too high in the military might create the false impression that decisions are open to debate, and might undermine discipline. It is a conceptual paper without new data. Its conclusion was to raise psychological safety while balancing it with discipline and accountability. It also writes that leading within a hierarchy is not the same as leading in an authoritarian way.

A 2026 study of 292 people working in a military hospital in Ghana found that psychological safety was linked, through trust in commanders, to thriving at work. What it measured as an outcome was thriving, not job performance. The view that a rank structure can suppress open dialogue is written as background in the introduction, and is not a result of the study.

Sources: Swain et al. (2024) Journal of Character & Leadership Development 11(2):55–67 (checked in the text. pp.55, 59, 62) / Nubuor et al. (2026) Frontiers in Psychiatry 17:1912314 (checked in the text)

Sources: Edmondson (1999) p.354 / Edmondson & Lei (2014) p.36, pp.39–41 / Helmreich, Merritt & Wilhelm (1999) International Journal of Aviation Psychology 9(1):19–32 (checked in the text of the authors' version)

So what should we do?

So far we have looked at why measures tend to miss the mark, one misunderstanding at a time. At the end of Part 1, we saw that depending on the stage of those who push them, measures tend to take the shape of a single "right answer." Then what should we do?

pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):One solution is to have the higher-order perspective of the stages of development through metacognition, and I thought that's why mindfulness and meditation have become popular. My aim is less relaxation or concentration than something like growth or purification at a more basic level.

pjdhiro's view of metacognition and stages of development also appears in the section of What I Want to Call Love-Driven Development (in Japanese) on how far theories and common sense work. The stability felt beyond metacognition is also written about on the same page (in Japanese). How metacognition and meditation relate to stages of development has not yet been checked against sources on this page.

There is also an argument that tried to bring attention to one's own reactions into a group's conversation. In 1991 Bohm and colleagues wrote a proposal for dialogue. What is placed at the core of dialogue is the suspension of thoughts, impulses and judgments. Suspension is said to be neither repressing reactions nor postponing them. It is bringing reactions, feelings and opinions out where they can be seen, within oneself and by others, and continuing to attend to them. They write that the movement of thought then slows down, and the structure of what we do automatically comes into view.

Bohm and colleagues also touch on the difficulty of holding dialogue in existing organizations. In an organization, they write, there may be a fear of voicing thoughts that could be seen as criticism of those above or of the culture's norms. So dialogue in an organization would begin by exploring the suspicion and fear that taking part arouses. They do not use the term psychological safety. Suspension runs in the opposite direction from a defense that keeps one from feeling anxiety, and in asking us to notice our reactions it can be read as close to the view of metacognition above (Claude's reading).

Sources: Bohm, Factor & Garrett (1991). Dialogue — A Proposal (checked in the text of a copy whose non-commercial distribution the authors permitted. Suspension: section "How to start a Dialogue"; fear in organizations: section "Dialogue in Existing Organizations")

There is also an argument that tried to make development part of the daily systems of the organization. In a 2016 book, the developmental psychologists Kegan, Lahey and colleagues wrote that in ordinary organizations many people are doing a "second job" that nobody pays them for: covering weaknesses, managing how they appear to others, hiding inadequacies, uncertainties and limitations. They also write that people are built to protect themselves, but equally built to grow psychologically.

The authors saw the three organizations taken up in the book as having created cultures that are safe enough and demanding enough, where everyone has stopped hiding, and called them Deliberately Developmental Organizations (DDOs). Theories of adult development are said to show the mechanisms by which, under such organizational practices, people bring to light, face, and move beyond the assumptions that bind them and their defensive routines. In placing safety and demand as a pair, this can be read as running in a different direction from the reading in Part 4, being kind, with no rigor (Claude's reading).

Sources: Kegan, Lahey, Fleming, Miller & Helsing (2016). An Everyone Culture: Becoming a Deliberately Developmental Organization. Harvard Business Review Press (checked in the text of the introduction placed on the site of The Developmental Edge, of which co-author Fleming is a co-founder. The second job p.1; protecting and growing p.2; DDO pp.3–4)

The military paper seen in Part 4's section "That higher is better and it is needed everywhere" concluded that psychological safety should be raised while being balanced with discipline and accountability. Taking up this balance, pjdhiro sees it as follows.

pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):Balance matters. I think psychological safety too is probably a one-sided cut, like the blind men and the elephant. In other words, I'm looking for its driving source. That's why I'm digging into "What is trust?"

The work of digging deep into trust is going on in Part 4 of What I Want to Call Love-Driven Development (in Japanese), in the form of comparing trust one by one with expectation, faith, idealization, assurance and dependence, and attachment.

Appendix

Open questions

This chapter lists the questions that, within the range we searched, have not been answered.

  • In military-type organizations that treat individuals like parts, does lower psychological safety work better?
  • How far do the view that it works in work dealing with intangible things and the review's angle, that it carries more weight in work with uncertainty that calls for creativity or collaboration, overlap?
  • Are there organizations that fit each stage?
  • Can speaking up in the direction of stopping errors and misconduct be handled with the same words as speaking up directed toward learning and creativity?
  • Did Schein and Bennis borrow the term "psychological safety" from Rogers?
  • What other findings complement the two viewpoints? For example, the psychology of shame and guilt, attachment, group dynamics.
  • How does the stage of development of those who push measures show in the shape of the measures and rules?
  • Do metacognition and meditation lead to having the higher-order perspective of the stages of development?
  • Does the state called high psychological safety overlap with a state of defense at the mature level?
  • After idealization peels away and satisfaction or psychological safety falls, what decides whether it heads toward breaking up or toward a safety like resignation?
