# What Is Psychological Safety

<p class="reader-summary__title">Overview</p>

<p class="reader-lead"><span class="reader-lead__label">Question</span>When people first come across the words "psychological safety," they take them to be about different things. Are these words connected with shame and anxiety?</p>

<a id="ps-stage-table"></a>The stages in the table below (Self-protective, Conformist, Expert…) are [stages of adult ego development](developmental-stages.html) (in Japanese). The same call to "raise psychological safety" may reach people as a request for different kinds of protection, depending on the stage of development of the person who hears it.

<details><summary>More — what a stage is</summary>

Some theories hold that ego development continues after we become adults. Cook-Greuter writes that on the path of adulthood, too, people may expand their awareness, stay where they are, or contract (p.2). She also writes that what changes from stage to stage is not the challenges people meet, but how they relate to them (p.7). What looks most threatening also differs by stage (pp.9–10).

The stage names in the table are Cook-Greuter's. People are said to move through the stages in order, from the top of the table downward, and the later stages can only be reached by passing through the earlier ones (p.2).

Cook-Greuter writes that, so that the stage names are not read as a ranking in which "higher is better," she speaks of "earlier" and "later" rather than "lower" and "higher" (p.7). She also writes that the stage descriptions are idealized, and are not to be seen as a simple staircase (p.4).

People are not fixed at one stage either. Nobody is 100% at one stage, and a person's responses are said to span at least three stages (p.5). People may also fall back to earlier stages under stress, or because of life circumstances and environment (pp.5, 7).

Stages are written about in more detail in [What Are Stages of Mental Development](developmental-stages.html) (in Japanese).

<div class="reader-lead"><span class="reader-lead__label">Current answer</span>
They are connected with shame and anxiety. Cook-Greuter writes that what becomes the main fear, and how people protect themselves, differ by <a href="#what-looks-threatening-changes-with-the-stage-of-development">stage of development</a>. pjdhiro sees a state without psychological safety as a state of <a href="#the-mind-has-ways-of-protecting-itself">defense against anxiety</a>, and places shame anxiety as one of the anxieties within it. On that basis, pjdhiro sees a state of <a href="#defenses-differ-in-maturity">mature defense</a> as what we call high psychological safety. Not being embarrassed for speaking up is part of the content of Edmondson's (1999) definition (<a href="#how-has-the-literature-on-psychological-safety-written-about-shame-anxiety">How has the literature on psychological safety written about shame anxiety?</a>). pjdhiro also sees that the reassurance and safety sought in a spirit of self-protection or opportunism can include a state like dependence produced by psychological defense mechanisms. Perhaps workplace measures so often miss the mark because of these differences in how the term is received: for example, taking it to mean that you are safe as long as you are not punished, that it can be created by declaring "Please say anything," or that it is about one's own reassurance and safety. We look at these one by one in the chapter <a href="#common-misunderstandings">Common misunderstandings</a>. The shape of measures and rules also tends to change with the stage of the people who push them; at the Conformist and Expert stages they can be read as tending to take the shape of a single "right answer" (<a href="#the-stage-of-those-who-push-the-measures">The stage of those who push the measures</a>).

### Stages of psychological safety

The best-known stages of psychological safety are the four stages put out by Clark and his company, LeaderFactor: inclusion safety, learner safety, contributor safety and challenger safety. People and groups are said to move through them in this order. The same company's technical report states that the answers to the 12 survey items came together as a single factor. In the answers, the four stages appeared as shades of a single dimension. The report has no data that followed the stages progressing in order over time. Nor did we find any peer-reviewed paper that tested these four stages.

Some research that followed teams over time does not fit the shape of climbing a staircase. In a study that measured 61 self-managed student teams (236 people) working on a five-month research project three times, at the beginning, middle and end, psychological safety fell from the beginning to the end.

pjdhiro overlaps this decline with what they have seen in close relationships.

<strong>pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):</strong>From my experience too, in close relationships, such as between spouses or friends, I often see mental growth like positive transference and negative transference. It isn't that expectations and ideals head in a straight line toward reassurance, wrapped in happiness. Defenses like idealization peel away, anxiety arises, and from there, by accepting reality, I think there is a psychological safety like resignation.

The authors of this study explain the decline by the stages of the work. At the beginning, many questions are allowed in order to grasp the task together. As the task comes into view, questions and comments are more easily taken as things that slow the work down. As the deadline nears, the team closes, and it becomes harder to reopen discussion. That is their explanation. They do not explain it as a process in which idealization peels away. Because the study measured three times over five months, whether safety comes back after falling was not followed.

<details><summary>More — how what comes after idealization peels away has been written about</summary>

In research that followed spouses from the time they were newly married, satisfaction also starts high and falls, on average. A study that followed 464 newlyweds for four years called this decline one of the most robust findings in research on marriage, and also showed that there are groups whose satisfaction does not fall (Lavner and Bradbury, 2010). In a study that followed 222 couples, married about half a year, for three years, satisfaction clearly fell overall, but those who strongly saw their partner as matching their own ideals did not show a fall in satisfaction (Murray et al., 2011). Idealization in that study is the degree to which people see their partner as matching an ideal image; it was not measured as a defense. In research on couples, the fading of idealization and the fall in satisfaction (disillusionment) have been studied as a forerunner of breaking up (Niehuis et al., 2013).

The psychoanalyst Klein wrote about the process of mourning in a form close to pjdhiro's view (1940). Idealization is a defense bound up with denial, and at the same time an intermediate step essential to the development of the mind. When hatred of the lost person arises, trust in the good object is shaken and idealization is painfully disturbed. Klein writes that as people gradually regain trust in external objects, they can bear to realize that the person was not perfect, and yet not lose trust and love. She also writes that painful experiences of every kind have something in common with mourning, and that meeting and overcoming adversity involves mental work similar to mourning. Klein's account is inferred from the clinical work with infants and with mourning; it does not measure couples or teams. Idealization itself is looked at in the Love-Driven Development chapter "[How do idealization and trust differ?](love-driven-dev.html#ldd-idealize)" (in Japanese).

pjdhiro's view places the disillusionment that research on couples has studied as a forerunner of breaking up on a path toward a psychological safety like resignation. We leave the two side by side, without fitting one to the other.

At the Conformist stage, rules are something to follow without question, and things are divided into right and wrong. At the Expert stage, one's own way looks like the only right way. Whichever of these stages the people pushing a measure are at, the measure can be read as tending to take the shape of a single "right answer." At the Pluralist stage the direction reverses: because every view is treated as equal, deciding tends to stall, Cook-Greuter writes.

On this page we have not come across research that directly examined the relation between the stage of those who push measures and the shape of the measures and rules. A close piece of research is the study by Rooke and Torbert. The two took part in the development efforts of ten organizations, about four years per organization, measured the CEOs' stage of development with a test, and set this beside where the organizations went. Torbert's stage names correspond one-to-one with the stages in the table above (Opportunist is Self-protective, Diplomat is Conformist, and Expert, Achiever and Strategist have the same names).

All five organizations with a CEO at the Strategist stage changed for the better. Of the five organizations with a CEO at an earlier stage, three fell into crisis. The other two changed for the better, and in both the CEO had a consultant or executive at the Strategist stage as a close adviser.

The authors also describe, stage by stage, how people face negative feedback. The Opportunist rejects feedback, and the Diplomat avoids conflict to protect their own and the other's face. The Expert accepts feedback only from masters in the same field, and the Achiever welcomes feedback that bears on goals. They also write that a Diplomat CEO would feel the threat of losing face in speaking of their own weaknesses in public.

What this study looked at was not the shape of measures and rules, but whether the organizations changed. It is also a retrospective look at ten organizations in which the authors themselves took part as consultants.

## History of the term

<!--OPEN-->

Part 2 begins by looking, in order of years, at where the term psychological safety came from. In this history, Schein writes of the forces that stop change as psychological defenses (the 1995 section).

Of the literature traced in this chapter, Schein's 1995 paper and Edmondson's 1999 paper were checked in the text. Lewin's 1947 paper was checked in a copy placed on the Internet Archive, and Rogers's 1954 essay in a copy, placed by a third party, of the full text reprinted in a 1970 collection. Schein and Bennis's book was checked only as far as the fragments found by a full-text search of the book.

### 1947: Lewin's three steps of change

The social psychologist Lewin described group change in three steps: unfreezing (if necessary) the present level, moving to a new level, and freezing at that level. Unfreezing is written about as a matter of the forces that loosen custom, overcoming the inner resistance of habit. The paper has no words that discuss safety of the mind or anxiety.

### 1954: Rogers's conditions for creativity

The term "psychological safety" was used by Rogers, of client-centered therapy, in his 1954 essay on creativity, as a condition under which creativity grows. It is said to be established by accepting the individual as of unconditional worth, by an absence of external evaluation, and by understanding empathically. Creativity here includes external products such as works, theories and inventions, and creating one's own personality anew in psychotherapy is counted as part of the same process. Rogers's interest in creativity came from that experience of psychotherapy.

### 1965: Unfreezing in Schein and Bennis

Schein and Bennis's book deals with training in which people learn in groups (laboratory training). Psychological safety is placed as one part of Lewin's unfreezing step, and is said to be created by reducing threat or by removing barriers to change. The book lays out several theories that explain learning in training. One is the interpersonal theory of Sullivan and Rogers; another is psychoanalysis, which deals with unconscious motives. But nothing in the fragments we could read shows that the term "psychological safety" was borrowed from Rogers. The chapter in the book by Bugental and Tannenbaum cites Maslow's distinction between deficiency motivation and growth motivation, and places sensitivity training on the side of growth.

pjdhiro reads these two ways of creating it from the side of psychological defense.

<strong>pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):</strong>Reducing threat is a state where psychological defense mechanisms aren't triggered; barriers to change means giving up psychological defense mechanisms and accepting reality. I think this has been reported a lot in counseling practice, by Shinya Nitta, Rogers and others. The methods and the difficulties.

The methods and difficulties, on the counseling side, of the process in which defenses loosen and reality is accepted are looked at in the chapter "Going back to defense, or keeping on facing it" of [What Is Anxiety](anxiety.html#守りに戻るか向き合い続けるか) (in Japanese).

### 1995: Schein looks back on Lewin

In 1995, in a paper looking back on Lewin's theory, Schein writes that he built his own theory of change on the foundation of Lewin's "unfreezing, changing, refreezing" model. These three words are Schein's phrasing; in Lewin's original they were unfreezing, moving and freezing. When Schein studied the attitude change of prisoners of war in the Korean War, he says, this model became the foundation for explaining the deep changes that had occurred in the prisoners. According to Schein, many of the forces that stop change are individual psychological defenses or group norms. He writes that he came to understand the whole of this only after turning to cognitive psychology's studies of perceptual defense, to psychoanalysis and the Tavistock work dealing with denial, splitting and projection, and to Argyris's research on defensive routines. Schein unpacked the unfreezing step into three processes and placed psychological safety as one of them. The three processes are receiving disconfirming information, the arousal of survival anxiety or guilt, and the creation of psychological safety. Without enough psychological safety, Schein writes, the information is denied or defended against in other ways, and no change occurs. The denial, splitting and projection that Schein lists are what psychoanalysis has called defense mechanisms, and they also appear in the table on the page [What Are Psychological Defense Mechanisms](defense-mechanisms.html#防衛の種類と水準) (in Japanese). The pair of learning anxiety and psychological safety is looked at later, in the chapter <a href="#how-has-the-literature-on-psychological-safety-written-about-shame-anxiety">How has the literature on psychological safety written about shame anxiety?</a>, through a paper on organizational culture from the same year.

pjdhiro sees this line of thought as also answering a question Shinya Nitta raised: "Are expectations bad?" Schein had written that information that disconfirms expectations becomes an entrance to change when there is enough psychological safety, and turns toward denial and defense when there is not. Nitta's question, and how people act after their expectations are disappointed, are looked at in the chapter "How do expectation and trust differ?" of [What I Want to Call Love-Driven Development](love-driven-dev.html#ldd-expect) (in Japanese).

### 1999: Edmondson's definition for teams

Edmondson defined team psychological safety and named Schein and Bennis's book as its root. Where she writes that admitting errors or asking for help carries the risk of losing face, she cites the sociologist Goffman's research on face. She does not cite Rogers.

### What the history tells us

Names from developmental psychology such as Erikson or Piaget do not appear in the literature traced here. Maslow's safety needs are not cited either.

To sum up what the history tells us: the term "psychological safety" has been used in different places with different contents, in therapy, in training where people learn in groups, in organizational change, and in research on teams. Between Rogers and Schein and Bennis, nothing has been found that shows the term was inherited. After 1999 too, the wording of the definition and the level at which it is measured keep moving (the section <a href="#that-you-are-safe-as-long-as-you-are-not-punished">That you are safe as long as you are not punished</a>). It is a term that spread without inheriting a single definition. Every author was writing on top of the experience of therapy, or of Lewin's theory and the research on prisoners.

The viewpoint of defense against anxiety overlaps with the language of defense that Schein took in from psychoanalysis. For the viewpoint of stages of development, no direct source can be seen in the history; it is a view that came from outside the history.

## How has the literature on psychological safety written about shame anxiety?

<!--OPEN-->

In pjdhiro's view, one of the anxieties that defenses try to protect against is shame anxiety. This chapter looks at how the literature on psychological safety has written about the anxiety of being shamed, in the order of Schein's two anxieties and Edmondson's definition for teams. At the end, we look at Brown, who spoke about shame and vulnerability from outside the literature on psychological safety.

### Schein's (1995) two anxieties

In a 1995 working paper on organizational culture, Schein paired two anxieties: the anxiety about learning something new (learning anxiety) and the anxiety that one will not survive without changing (survival anxiety). Psychological safety is placed as the first condition for lowering learning anxiety: feeling that one can learn something new without losing one's sense of who one is or one's sense of competence. He also writes that when learning anxiety stays high, people become defensive, deny reality, rationalize, and blame others. Schein's view is that survival anxiety is usually already high enough, because people are told daily that their old habits no longer fit present reality, and that it is learning anxiety that should be lowered.

### Edmondson's (1999) definition for teams

It was Edmondson's 1999 paper that set psychological safety as a research term. It is defined as "a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking."

The paper places the root of the idea in Schein and Bennis's 1965 book. Edmondson summarizes it as a book that argued for the need to create psychological safety so that people feel secure and capable of changing. Where the term came from was looked at in the chapter <a href="#history-of-the-term">History of the term</a> at the start of Part 2.

The confidence that the team will not embarrass someone for speaking up is part of the content of the 1999 definition. The paper writes that admitting errors or asking for help carries the risk of being seen as incompetent and losing face, so people tend to hold back from such behavior.

Alongside this, the same confidence includes not being rejected and not being punished. The paper has no word referring to physical safety. What it writes of as protected is protection from embarrassment, rejection and punishment, and being oneself, a sense of competence, and self-esteem.

The confidence that one will not be embarrassed, rejected or punished is said to stem from mutual respect and trust. Psychological safety is also described as something that "involves but goes beyond interpersonal trust" and as a climate in which "people are comfortable being themselves." In the paper's discussion it is restated as a climate mixing trust, respect for each other's competence, and caring about each other as people. Edmondson's definition wraps not being punished, and being able to be oneself in respect and trust, within a single paragraph.

The definition is not an individual's feeling but a belief shared by the team. The paper also writes that this belief is usually tacit, "taken for granted."

The paper sets up and tests the hypothesis that team psychological safety promotes learning behavior. The examples of learning behavior it gives are seeking feedback, sharing information, asking for help, talking about errors, and experimenting.

How this confidence tends to be received in a shrunken form in the workplace is looked at in <a href="#common-misunderstandings">Common misunderstandings</a> in Part 4.

### Brown's (2010) shame and vulnerability

One person who has spoken about shame from outside the literature on psychological safety is Brown, a qualitative researcher with a degree in social work. What we cite here is a 2010 talk in which she spoke about her own research, not a research paper.

In the talk, Brown says shame is easily understood as the fear of disconnection: the fear that if people know or see some part of us, we will not be worthy of connection. In Edmondson's paper, the risk of embarrassment was written of as the risk of being seen as incompetent and losing face. Brown's way of putting it places the weight on falling out of relationships.

Brown says that vulnerability lies at the core of shame and fear and of the struggle over whether we are worthy. At the same time, she says, it also seems to be where joy, creativity, belonging and love are born. What the people with a strong sense of worthiness had in common was that they embraced vulnerability, and she says that we usually numb vulnerability. This move of not feeling vulnerability can also be read as one of the <a href="#the-mind-has-ways-of-protecting-itself">defenses</a> (Claude's reading).

## Defenses differ in maturity

<!--OPEN-->

The view of pjdhiro quoted in the overview sees a state of mature defense as what we call high psychological safety. This chapter looks at what can be said about the maturity of defenses from this page's sources: the arrangement of the kinds and levels of defense; how immature defenses relate to personality disorders and workplace bullying; and how much defense is needed, in that order.

As we saw in the chapter <a href="#the-mind-has-ways-of-protecting-itself">The mind has ways of protecting itself</a>, Vaillant distinguished "mature" defenses such as sublimation and humor from "immature" defenses such as projection, and held that which one uses makes a great difference to mental health. He also writes that when anxiety and loneliness are eased by empathy, "immature" defenses can come undone.

In the table of stages of development in Part 1, the later the stage, the more mature the defenses listed. In the Self-protective row are fantasy, acting out and projection; in the Strategist row, suppression, positive reframing, altruism and non-hostile humor; in the Construct-aware and later row, sublimation, non-hostile humor and bracketing. Cook-Greuter also writes that the later the stage, the less need there is for defensive moves.

### Kinds and levels of defense

The table arranging the kinds and levels of defense is on the page [What Are Psychological Defense Mechanisms](defense-mechanisms.html#防衛の種類と水準) (in Japanese). Perry's Defense Mechanism Rating Scales arrange 30 defenses into seven levels and group them into three categories: immature, neurotic and mature. Each level is described by what it protects the mind from. The level that protects against the imminent threat of being punished or abandoned, and the level that protects self-esteem from the feelings of shame or helplessness produced by failure or criticism, are in the immature category. The most mature level is described as taking in stress without distortion, acknowledging one's own limits, and relying on help when it is available.

### Immature defenses, personality disorders and workplace bullying

Vaillant writes that immature defenses are common in personality disorders, and produce behavior that looks irritating to those around. There is also research that depicts workplace bullying as defenses such as splitting and projection operating among the people who bully, the people who are bullied, and the organization. The figures and limits of that research, and a view that sets the severity and type of personality side by side, are looked at on the page [What Are Psychological Defense Mechanisms](defense-mechanisms.html#未熟な防衛は周りの人にどう現れるか) (in Japanese).

A paper reviewing research on the perpetration of bullying writes, citing earlier studies, that when a workplace's psychosocial safety climate (a shared perception that management prioritizes workers' psychological health) is strong, opportunities for aggression are held down. Psychosocial safety climate is defined as a concept separate from Edmondson's psychological safety (the belief that it is safe to take interpersonal risks). On the relation between the two, pjdhiro sees it as follows.

<strong>pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):</strong>I think they're pointing at much the same thing. Because the individual and the organization interact.

The relation between a person's defenses and the strength of the threat in the setting is looked at later, in the section <a href="#how-much-defense-is-needed">How much defense is needed</a>.

### How much defense is needed

If defenses differ in maturity, how much defense is needed should differ too. From the name of the term itself, pjdhiro raises the question as follows.

<strong>pjdhiro's question (translated by Claude):</strong>Going by the term psychological safety itself, you can infer both defending for the sake of safety, and being in a state that is safe even without defending, can't you?

In Perry's rating scales, the most mature level is described as taking in internal and external stress without distortion, acknowledging one's own limits, and relying on help when it is available. The more immature the level, the more stress is attributed to the outside as unbearable, parts of reality are not acknowledged, and images of oneself and others are distorted. The degrees of maturity are arranged as degrees of how far a threat can be taken in without distortion.

Even so, the mature level is also counted as a defense. Vaillant writes that even the most pathological defenses calm the mind, and sees defenses as coping responses, like fever. The sources we could read do not depict a state without defense. Brought close to the sources' language, "a state that is safe even without defending" can be read as close to the mature level at which a threat can be taken in without distortion.

Which level of defense appears also depends on the strength of the threat. Vaillant writes that when empathy eases anxiety and loneliness, immature defenses fall apart. Perry's table also counts confiding in and relying on others as a mature defense. In research on workplace bullying, supervisors were more likely to abuse subordinates when their own identity was threatened, and job insecurity also intensified aggression. Schein writes that balancing the amount of threat produced by disconfirming information with enough psychological safety to take it in is the key to moving change forward (<a href="#1995-schein-looks-back-on-lewin">the 1995 section of the history of the term</a>). In this argument, psychological safety is placed as what lets the information be taken in without defending against it.

This page reads the degree to which defense is needed as changing with both the strength of the threat in the setting and a person's ability to take in the threat without distortion. The view that a state of mature defense is what we call high psychological safety can be read as pointing to the mature end of this arrangement. We have not yet come across research that measured psychological safety and level of defense side by side in the same people or teams.

## How do trust and psychological safety differ?

<!--OPEN-->

In 1999 Edmondson wrote that psychological safety "involves but goes beyond interpersonal trust." Then where do the two differ?

<a id="ps-trust-levels"></a>In a 2002 working paper, Edmondson writes of the difference from trust on three points: time frame, focus, and level of analysis. Psychological safety looks at the interpersonal consequences in the very near term. Trust is a matter of whether one gives others the benefit of the doubt. Psychological safety is a matter of whether others will give oneself the benefit of the doubt, and its focus is on oneself. A team member's words are quoted: "I don't have to wear a mask in this team…it's easy to be myself." Trust is said to concern mainly the relationship between two people, and psychological safety the group.

Edmondson and Lei's 2014 review describes psychological safety as "people's perceptions of the consequences of taking interpersonal risks in a particular context such as a workplace." It also arranges the research into three levels: measured as an individual's experience, measured as a group's climate, and measured as an organization's average.

The 1999 definition held that the confidence that one will not be embarrassed, rejected or punished stems from mutual respect and trust ([How has the literature on psychological safety written about shame anxiety?](#how-has-the-literature-on-psychological-safety-written-about-shame-anxiety)). <strong>pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):</strong>If it's rapport or trust, those are difficult concepts, difficult enough that Love-Driven Development is searching for them, so I think plain, simple words like reassurance and safety lead to a lot of misunderstanding.

## Common misunderstandings

<!--OPEN-->

This chapter looks at how psychological safety tends to be received in the workplace, and at the phenomena from which those readings arise.

The first four are shifts that occur on the side of being protected from shame and anxiety. The last two do not fit either viewpoint; they are shifts about how it is created and the conditions under which it works. The shift visible from the side of stages is what we saw at the end of Part 1: depending on the stage of those who push them, measures tend to take the shape of a single "right answer." That a difficult concept such as trust is received through plain words like reassurance and safety may also be one of the foundations of these shifts (pjdhiro's view quoted in the chapter [How do trust and psychological safety differ?](#how-do-trust-and-psychological-safety-differ)).

### That safety means physical safety

The word "safety" is used, in everyday life and in the fields of accidents and health, to refer to physical safety. Some research in sport includes not being physically harmed in the definition.

Read toward physical safety, psychological safety sounds like something close to the basic needs for staying alive.

<strong>pjdhiro's question (translated by Claude):</strong>It's psychologically safe, not biologically safe, right? Hasn't the nuance shifted, before we noticed, to taking care only of lower-order needs?

In Japanese, there are examples of academic commentaries that render "safe" in the original as "anshin shite" (feeling reassured). The three Japanese academic commentaries we checked appeared in journals of safety engineering, of quality and safety in health care, and of occupational health. All three journals belong to fields concerned with preventing accidents and harm to health. However, we did not find material that directly supports the view that the Japanese word "anzen" (safety) pulls the reading toward physical safety. Where the commentaries appeared remains only a situational clue.

In the papers on psychological safety we checked, no word refers to physical safety. What the 1999 definition writes of as protected was looked at in the chapter [How has the literature on psychological safety written about shame anxiety?](#how-has-the-literature-on-psychological-safety-written-about-shame-anxiety)

### That you are safe as long as you are not punished

A system of punishment is visible, and it is easy to check whether one has put it in place.

Within research, too, the wording of the definition keeps moving. A 2022 paper reviewing research in sport pointed out that the definitions used in sport have been rewritten into the perception of being protected from psychological harm, or of not being physically harmed and being accepted and respected. It also writes that what was set as a group belief has turned into an individual-level concept.

On the side of measurement, too, what is pointed to is not aligned. Of the 153 quantitative studies counted by a 2023 review, 40 measured at the individual level, 79 at the group level, 28 at the organizational level, and 6 did not state the level. The review's authors themselves write that there are small differences in conceptualization and measurement. In a study of health-care teams, there is an example of questions about the team being reworded into a form that asks for individual evaluation.

The 1999 definition writes, alongside not being punished, of not being embarrassed, not being rejected, and being able to be oneself in respect and trust.

On where the misreading occurs when it is read toward punishment, pjdhiro sees it as follows.

<strong>pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):</strong>Edmondson puts weight on psychological defense against weakness and wretchedness. I think what causes the misunderstanding is that some people, from the word safety, take it at the entrance to be a matter of wrongdoing, sin, gratitude or apology.

The view that separates wrongdoing from weakness is one pjdhiro took from Shinya Nitta's way of dividing them ([What Is Anxiety](anxiety.html), in Japanese).

Edmondson's 1999 paper places the reasons why people do not admit errors or ask for help in appearing incompetent, damaging their image, and losing face. Punishment, too, she writes of in the form of well-intentioned interpersonal risks not being punished. The paper has no words for sin, apology or wrongdoing. However, the word the paper uses is not shame but embarrass.

Research on emotion has distinguished shame, in which one's whole self is negated, from guilt, in which a specific act is negated. Shame is said to be bound up with hiding, denying and turning blame outward; guilt with apologizing and making amends. The two can be distinguished, but it is also written that when guilt spreads to the whole self, it can fuse with shame. Defenses that protect self-esteem from shame are, in the table on the page [What Are Psychological Defense Mechanisms](defense-mechanisms.html#防衛の種類と水準) (in Japanese), in the immature category, at the level of minor image-distorting defenses.

On the closeness of shame and guilt, pjdhiro sees it as follows.

<strong>pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):</strong>Shame and guilt are as closely linked as two sides of a coin. I think that is something that could even be a hint at the very essence of the mind, but here, an emotion that exists as something non-dual is one aspect, and it is strictly something at the level of consciousness. For example, I think a person who is angry is angry without knowing, even themselves, whether it is a lid on sadness, a defense against wretchedness, or a way of coping with the anxiety that the defense is about to peel away.

The directions of shrinking may include one that leans toward being protected from harm and one that moves from a group belief to the individual level; perhaps there is not just one direction.

### That it means being kind, with no rigor

Friendliness and ease of speaking up are qualities easy to see from the outside. In 1999 Edmondson wrote that psychological safety is neither a careless permissiveness nor a constantly positive feeling. In 2002 she wrote that it is not a cozy setting in which members are necessarily close friends, nor the absence of pressure or problems.

Sources: [Edmondson (1999)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999) p.354 / [Edmondson (2002)](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/02-062_0b5726a8-443d-4629-9e75-736679b870fc.pdf) p.7

Argyris wrote of the way kindness stops learning as an organizational defense. In a 1987 paper he defined organizational defensive routines as policies or actions that keep individuals or organizations from experiencing threat or embarrassment, and at the same time prevent them from finding and reducing its causes. He also writes that their source lies in social virtues taught early in life.

The example he gives is the premise that people should be caring and supportive. Under this premise, Argyris writes, caring and support are taken to mean saying what the other can hear and wants to hear. As a result, he says, roundabout handling, and messages in which what is said and what is shown do not match, increase.

### That it is about one's own reassurance and safety

In the section on physical safety, we saw examples of Japanese commentaries that render "safe" as "feeling reassured." Attention also turns to whose reassurance it is taken to be.

<strong>pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):</strong>Isn't everyone conveniently interpreting it as "my own reassurance and safety"? In order to act selfishly.

The 1999 definition is not an individual's feeling but a belief shared by the team. It was studied as something that promotes behavior through which a team learns (asking for help, talking about errors, and so on). Reading it only as being protected oneself can be read as dropping both of these.

The shrinking from a group belief to the individual level, seen in the section [That you are safe as long as you are not punished](#that-you-are-safe-as-long-as-you-are-not-punished), may lie just before this reading. The reassurance and safety sought in a spirit of self-protection or opportunism (the chapter [The mind has ways of protecting itself](#the-mind-has-ways-of-protecting-itself)) can also be read as a matter running in the same direction.

### That it can be created by declaring "Please say anything"

A declaration is a visible move that can be made at once. One Japanese commentary writes that fostering a team's psychological safety is not only a matter of setting up a peaceful environment where people can speak with reassurance.

The 1999 paper has a sentence saying that even when a team's tacit beliefs are sometimes discussed explicitly, the content of psychological safety does not change. That is as far as the original goes; it does not touch on whether it can be created by declaration. In the definition, this belief is usually tacit, and is a confidence that stems from respect and trust.

### That higher is better and it is needed everywhere

Much research shows links with learning and performance, and it has been widely introduced. On the other hand, the 2014 review raises the possibility that, over time, people become too comfortable with each other and the emphasis drifts away from work and learning. It also writes that excessive psychological safety may lead people to spend time on things that do not matter, or to lose the motivation to learn.

Then is psychological safety needed in the same way in every kind of work and organization?

<strong>pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):</strong>In military-type organizations that treat individuals like parts, I think psychological safety is low, but that may work better. I think it's effective when dealing with intangible things like services and design (just a hunch).

The research side also has clues about the conditions under which it works. Edmondson's 1999 paper writes that, in very routine, repetitive work, learning behavior can lower efficiency and harm performance. At the same time, it goes on to say that even teams doing routine production need learning behavior for self-management and occasional process improvement. The 2014 review wrote that psychological safety carries more weight when there is uncertainty and creativity or collaboration is needed. The reason given is that without elements of uncertainty or collaboration, the need to face interpersonal risk is not prominent in the first place.

The review's angle is whether there is uncertainty and whether creativity or collaboration is needed; it does not cut by tangible versus intangible. Much work in services and design can be read as meeting this condition. Some work, such as research and development or surgery, deals with tangible things and still meets the condition, and some work, such as counter procedures, is intangible and routine. The two angles overlap a great deal, but are not the same.

Within the range we could check, we found no research that measured psychological safety and directly showed that performance was better when it was lower.

Then can the same be said of situations where errors or misconduct must be stopped?

<strong>pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):</strong>If you're talking in the context of compliance, misconduct or governance, that would be different, though.

The research so far has looked at speaking up directed toward learning and creativity. Speaking up in the direction of stopping and reporting errors and dangers seems, judging from the example of aviation, to have been treated as needed regardless of the nature of the work. Crew resource management training in aviation began by correcting the lack of assertiveness of junior crew members and the authoritarian behavior of captains, and later moved toward treating error as normal and managing it. Training in the leadership role that comes with command was also created for new captains.

## So what should we do?

<!--OPEN-->

So far we have looked at why measures tend to miss the mark, one misunderstanding at a time. At the end of Part 1, we saw that depending on the stage of those who push them, measures tend to take the shape of a single "right answer." Then what should we do?

<strong>pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):</strong>One solution is to have the higher-order perspective of the stages of development through metacognition, and I thought that's why mindfulness and meditation have become popular. My aim is less relaxation or concentration than something like growth or purification at a more basic level.

pjdhiro's view of metacognition and stages of development also appears in the section of [What I Want to Call Love-Driven Development](love-driven-dev.html#ldd-use-3) (in Japanese) on how far theories and common sense work. The stability felt beyond metacognition is also written about [on the same page](love-driven-dev.html#ldd-love-umbilical) (in Japanese). How metacognition and meditation relate to stages of development has not yet been checked against sources on this page.

There is also an argument that tried to bring attention to one's own reactions into a group's conversation. In 1991 Bohm and colleagues wrote a proposal for dialogue. What is placed at the core of dialogue is the suspension of thoughts, impulses and judgments. Suspension is said to be neither repressing reactions nor postponing them. It is bringing reactions, feelings and opinions out where they can be seen, within oneself and by others, and continuing to attend to them. They write that the movement of thought then slows down, and the structure of what we do automatically comes into view.

Bohm and colleagues also touch on the difficulty of holding dialogue in existing organizations. In an organization, they write, there may be a fear of voicing thoughts that could be seen as criticism of those above or of the culture's norms. So dialogue in an organization would begin by exploring the suspicion and fear that taking part arouses. They do not use the term psychological safety. Suspension runs in the opposite direction from a defense that keeps one from feeling anxiety, and in asking us to notice our reactions it can be read as close to the view of metacognition above (Claude's reading).

Sources: Bohm, Factor & Garrett (1991). *Dialogue — A Proposal* (checked in the text of a [copy](https://www.capacitie.org/content/dialogue/DialogueProposal.pdf) whose non-commercial distribution the authors permitted. Suspension: section "How to start a Dialogue"; fear in organizations: section "Dialogue in Existing Organizations")

There is also an argument that tried to make development part of the daily systems of the organization. In a 2016 book, the developmental psychologists Kegan, Lahey and colleagues wrote that in ordinary organizations many people are doing a "second job" that nobody pays them for: covering weaknesses, managing how they appear to others, hiding inadequacies, uncertainties and limitations. They also write that people are built to protect themselves, but equally built to grow psychologically.

The authors saw the three organizations taken up in the book as having created cultures that are safe enough and demanding enough, where everyone has stopped hiding, and called them Deliberately Developmental Organizations (DDOs). Theories of adult development are said to show the mechanisms by which, under such organizational practices, people bring to light, face, and move beyond the assumptions that bind them and their defensive routines. In placing safety and demand as a pair, this can be read as running in a different direction from the reading in Part 4, being kind, with no rigor (Claude's reading).

Sources: Kegan, Lahey, Fleming, Miller & Helsing (2016). *An Everyone Culture: Becoming a Deliberately Developmental Organization*. Harvard Business Review Press (checked in the text of the [introduction](https://developmentaledge.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/An-Everyone-Culture-Introduction-Chapter.pdf) placed on the site of The Developmental Edge, of which co-author Fleming is a co-founder. The second job p.1; protecting and growing p.2; DDO pp.3–4)

The military paper seen in Part 4's section "That higher is better and it is needed everywhere" concluded that psychological safety should be raised while being balanced with discipline and accountability. Taking up this balance, pjdhiro sees it as follows.

<strong>pjdhiro's view (translated by Claude):</strong>Balance matters. I think psychological safety too is probably a one-sided cut, like the blind men and the elephant. In other words, I'm looking for its driving source. That's why I'm digging into "What is trust?"

The work of digging deep into trust is going on in Part 4 of [What I Want to Call Love-Driven Development](love-driven-dev.html#信頼と信仰や期待を人は自分の中で分けられるのか) (in Japanese), in the form of comparing trust one by one with expectation, faith, idealization, assurance and dependence, and attachment.

## Open questions

This chapter lists the questions that, within the range we searched, have not been answered.

- In military-type organizations that treat individuals like parts, does lower psychological safety work better?
- How far do the view that it works in work dealing with intangible things and the review's angle, that it carries more weight in work with uncertainty that calls for creativity or collaboration, overlap?
- Are there organizations that fit each stage?
- Can speaking up in the direction of stopping errors and misconduct be handled with the same words as speaking up directed toward learning and creativity?
- Did Schein and Bennis borrow the term "psychological safety" from Rogers?
- What other findings complement the two viewpoints? For example, the psychology of shame and guilt, attachment, group dynamics.
- How does the stage of development of those who push measures show in the shape of the measures and rules?
- Do metacognition and meditation lead to having the higher-order perspective of the stages of development?
- Does the state called high psychological safety overlap with a state of defense at the mature level?
- After idealization peels away and satisfaction or psychological safety falls, what decides whether it heads toward breaking up or toward a safety like resignation?
